Beneficiaries

Choosing Life Insurance Beneficiaries: A Practical Guide

By the Smart Life Insurance Quote team · Last updated 23 September 2026

Educational information, not professional advice. Policies and rules vary by insurer and state; check details with a licensed professional.

The beneficiary form is often the shortest page in a life insurance application, and one of the most important. It decides who receives the money, how quickly, and sometimes whether it goes through court first. A few minutes of careful thought here can save your family months of hassle later.

Primary and contingent beneficiaries

Your primary beneficiary is the person or entity first in line to receive the death benefit. You can name more than one and split the payout by percentage, as long as the shares add up to 100 percent. Your contingent beneficiary (sometimes called secondary) receives the money only if every primary beneficiary has died before you or cannot be found. Always name at least one contingent. Without one, if your primary beneficiary dies first, the money may default to your estate.

Who or what you can name

  • Individuals, such as a spouse, partner, adult child, sibling or friend. Use full legal names and, where the form asks, dates of birth or other identifying details.
  • A trust, which lets a trustee manage and distribute money according to rules you set. This is common when children are young or a beneficiary has special needs.
  • A charity, using its full legal name and, ideally, its tax identification number.
  • Your estate, which is possible but usually means the money goes through probate, can be delayed and may become available to creditors.

Why naming minor children directly causes problems

Insurers generally will not pay a large sum directly to a minor. If you name a young child, a court may need to appoint a guardian of the child's property to manage the money, which takes time and costs money, and the child may receive everything outright at the age of majority. Common alternatives include naming a trust for the children's benefit, or naming a custodian under your state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act if the insurer allows it. An estate-planning attorney can explain which option fits your state and family.

Per stirpes and per capita, in plain terms

These Latin phrases answer one question: if one of your beneficiaries dies before you, where does their share go?

Not every insurer offers both choices on the form, and wording varies. If this matters to your family, ask the insurer how it would handle each scenario.

Special situations to check

Marriage and community property. In community property states, a spouse may have rights to part of a policy bought with marital funds, and some insurers ask for spousal consent if you name someone else. Divorce. Some states automatically revoke an ex-spouse's designation after divorce, others do not, and employer plans can follow different federal rules. Do not rely on automatic rules; update the form. Blended families. If you want children from a previous relationship to receive a share, name them explicitly or use a trust, rather than assuming a new spouse will pass money on.

Revocable versus irrevocable. Most designations are revocable, meaning you can change them at any time. An irrevocable beneficiary must consent to changes, which can be required by a divorce settlement or used in some estate plans.

When to review your beneficiaries

Review your form after any of these

  • Marriage, divorce or a new partnership
  • The birth or adoption of a child or grandchild
  • The death of a named beneficiary
  • Creating a will or trust, or changing one
  • Starting or leaving a job with group life cover
  • A beneficiary developing special needs or financial difficulties

Keep a copy of the confirmation the insurer sends you, and let your beneficiaries know that the policy exists and which company holds it. A payout cannot help anyone who does not know to claim it.

Frequently asked questions

Does my will override my life insurance beneficiary?

Generally no. The beneficiary designation on the policy usually controls who receives the death benefit, regardless of what your will says.

Can I name my partner if we are not married?

Yes, in most cases you can name anyone. The insurer may ask about the relationship to confirm an insurable interest when you apply.

What happens if I do not name any beneficiary?

The death benefit is often paid to your estate, which can mean probate delays, costs and exposure to creditors.

Can I split the payout between several people?

Yes. You can assign percentages to multiple primary beneficiaries, as long as they add up to 100 percent.
