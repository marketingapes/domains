How Much Life Insurance Do I Need? Three Ways to Estimate It

By the Smart Life Insurance Quote team · Last updated 23 September 2026

Educational information, not professional advice. Policies and rules vary by insurer and state; check details with a licensed professional.

"How much cover should I get?" is the question that stalls most people. There is no single correct number, but there are sensible ways to get from a vague worry to a figure you can price. This guide walks through three methods, from quickest to most thorough, and the adjustments people commonly forget.

Start with the job the money has to do

Life insurance is money that arrives when an income or a caregiver disappears. So instead of asking "how much insurance", ask "what would my household need to pay for, and for how long, if I were not here?" Typical jobs include clearing debts, keeping a roof over everyone's head, replacing years of income, paying for childcare that you currently provide, funding education, and covering funeral and estate costs. Some people also want to leave a gift or legacy. List the jobs first; the maths follows.

Method 1: the income multiple

The quickest rule of thumb is to multiply your annual income by a number, often somewhere between 10 and 15. It is easy and it gets you into the right neighbourhood for many working-age households. Its weaknesses are obvious once you look: it ignores your debts, how many dependants you have, how old they are, what you have already saved and whether your partner earns. Two people on the same salary can have wildly different needs. Treat an income multiple as a sanity check, not an answer.

Method 2: DIME

DIME is a simple checklist that adds up four common needs. It is the method used in the estimator on our home page, and it is a good middle ground between speed and accuracy.

Add the four together, then subtract resources that would already be available: existing life insurance (including cover through work, while you remain employed there) and savings or investments your family could use. The result is a rough target.

A worked example with round numbers

Imagine a parent earning 60,000 a year with two young children. They have 15,000 of car and card debt and set aside 10,000 for final expenses, so D is 25,000. They want to replace income for 15 years, so I is 900,000. The mortgage balance is 250,000. They would like to put aside 50,000 per child for education, so E is 100,000. That totals 1,275,000. They have 120,000 of group cover at work and 30,000 in savings, which brings the estimate down to about 1,125,000. The figures are purely illustrative, but the process is exactly the same with your own.

Method 3: a fuller needs analysis

A needs analysis refines DIME by looking at cash flow rather than lump sums. Instead of replacing your whole income, you estimate what the household would actually spend without you, subtract what a surviving partner would still earn, and consider any survivor benefits your family might receive. You then think about how many years that gap lasts and whether the lump sum would be invested. This approach can raise or lower the number significantly. It is also the method most financial planners use, and it is the right time to talk to one if your situation is complicated.

Adjustments people often forget

  • Unpaid work. A stay-at-home parent's childcare, driving and household management would cost real money to replace.
  • Inflation. Fifteen years of income today will buy less in year fifteen. Some people add a buffer.
  • Workplace cover is not permanent. Group life usually ends or must be converted if you leave the job.
  • Debts that disappear on death. Some debts, such as many federal student loans in the United States, are discharged when the borrower dies, so you may not need to insure them. Private and co-signed loans are different.
  • Timing. Needs shrink as the mortgage is paid down and children grow up. Laddering two or more term policies of different lengths can match that shape.

From a number to a quote

Once you have an estimate, round it to a figure insurers commonly offer and pick a term that covers the longest of your needs. Then price a few scenarios, for example the full amount, a smaller amount and a ladder of two policies. Seeing the options side by side makes it much easier to decide what is comfortable. Remember that insurers decide the maximum they will offer based on your income, age and finances, so a very large figure may need justification during underwriting.

Frequently asked questions

Is ten times my salary enough life insurance?

It can be a reasonable starting point, but it ignores debts, dependants, savings and your partner's income. Use a method like DIME to check it.

Should I include my mortgage if my partner could keep paying it?

Many people still include it, because clearing the mortgage reduces pressure on a single income. It depends on what you want the money to achieve.

Do I need life insurance if I have no dependants?

Often much less, or none. Some single people still buy a small policy to cover debts, funeral costs or a co-signed loan.

Does the DIME estimate on this site tell me what to buy?

No. It is a rough educational estimate to help you think. A licensed professional can look at your full situation.
