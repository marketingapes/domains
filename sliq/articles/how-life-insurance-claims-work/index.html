How Life Insurance Claims Work: A Calm Guide for Beneficiaries

By the Smart Life Insurance Quote team · Last updated 23 September 2026

Educational information, not professional advice. Policies and rules vary by insurer and state; check details with a licensed professional.

If you are reading this because someone has died, we are sorry. Claiming life insurance is usually more straightforward than people fear, and there is rarely a need to rush in the first days. This guide explains the steps, the paperwork and the choices you may be offered, so you know what to expect.

Step 1: find the policy and the insurer

Look for a policy document, annual statements, premium payments on bank statements, or correspondence from an insurer. Check with the person's employer, since many people have group cover through work, and with any union or professional association they belonged to. Financial advisers and attorneys may also know. If you believe a policy exists but cannot find it, the NAIC's free Life Insurance Policy Locator lets you submit a request that participating insurers search for. Unclaimed benefits may also be reported to state unclaimed property programmes.

Step 2: contact the insurer

Call the insurer's claims department or start a claim online. You will usually need the policy number if you have it, the insured person's full name, date of birth and Social Security number, and the date and cause of death. The insurer will confirm who the named beneficiaries are and send a claim form. Each beneficiary typically files separately.

Step 3: gather the documents

Documents insurers commonly request

  • A completed claim form (sometimes called a claimant's statement)
  • A certified copy of the death certificate; ordering several copies helps with other paperwork too
  • Your own identification and tax information
  • The policy document, if you have it (many insurers can proceed without it)
  • Additional papers in some cases, such as a coroner's report or medical records
  • Letters of administration or court papers if the estate is the beneficiary

Step 4: review and payment

Once the insurer has everything it needs, straightforward claims are often paid within weeks. Many states require insurers to pay within a set time after receiving proof of death, and some require interest on delayed payments. Claims can take longer if the death occurred during the contestability period, which is usually the first two years of the policy. During that time the insurer may review the original application to confirm it was accurate. Most policies also contain a suicide clause, commonly two years, during which the benefit may be limited to a refund of premiums. After those periods, the grounds for denying a claim are much narrower.

Choosing how to receive the money

There is no need to decide immediately what to do with a large sum. Many people park the money somewhere safe and accessible while they grieve, then make decisions later, ideally with a fee-only financial planner or other trusted professional.

Taxes, briefly

In the United States, life insurance death benefits paid to a named beneficiary are generally not subject to federal income tax. Interest earned on the money, for example if payment is delayed or held in an account, is usually taxable. Estate tax can apply to very large estates, and ownership arrangements can affect this. Rules differ in other countries and for unusual situations, so check with a tax professional if you are unsure.

If a claim is delayed or denied

Ask the insurer for the reason in writing and for a copy of the documents it relied on. Many disputes come down to missing paperwork that can be supplied. If you believe a denial is wrong, you can use the insurer's appeal process and file a complaint with your state insurance department. For large or complex disputes, an attorney who handles insurance claims can advise you.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a life insurance payout take?

Straightforward claims are often paid within a few weeks of the insurer receiving all documents. Deaths within the contestability period may take longer.

Do I need a lawyer to claim life insurance?

Usually not for a straightforward claim by a named beneficiary. Legal help can be useful if the claim is denied or the estate is the beneficiary.

Is there a deadline to file a claim?

Policies often ask for prompt notice, but valid claims are generally still paid later. It is still best to file when you are able.

What if I cannot find the policy document?

Contact the insurer anyway. They can usually locate the policy with the insured person's details. The NAIC policy locator can help if you do not know the insurer.
