Life Insurance for Stay-at-Home Parents: Valuing Unpaid Work

By the Smart Life Insurance Quote team · Last updated 23 September 2026

Educational information, not professional advice. Policies and rules vary by insurer and state; check details with a licensed professional.

When families think about life insurance, they often focus on the parent who earns a salary. But if a stay-at-home parent died, the family would still need someone to do everything that parent does: childcare, school runs, meals, appointments, laundry and household management. Replacing that work costs money, often a lot of it, and life insurance can provide it.

Why a stay-at-home parent needs cover

A surviving working parent might need to pay for full-time childcare, after-school care, cleaning help or meal services. They might need to reduce working hours, turn down travel, or take unpaid leave for a while. Grief itself can make work harder for months. Without insurance, the family may have to dip into savings or take on debt precisely when life is most disrupted. Cover on the stay-at-home parent is about keeping the household running.

Estimating the value of unpaid work

There is no salary to multiply, so build the number from tasks. Use local prices you can find easily, such as childcare centre fees or nanny rates in your area, rather than national averages.

Add up the yearly costs, multiply by the number of years, then add any debts, final expenses and education goals using the DIME approach. The result is often surprisingly large, which is the point of the exercise. It shows the real financial contribution that does not appear on a payslip.

What insurers may ask

Insurers want the amount of cover to be reasonable. For applicants without earned income, many base the maximum on the working spouse's income or cover, and some limit a non-working spouse to a proportion of the earner's policy or to a set maximum. The exact rules differ between companies and change over time. If you are applying for a large amount, expect questions about household income and the reason for the cover. Being ready to explain the childcare and household costs you have estimated helps.

Choosing the type and term

For most stay-at-home parents, the heaviest cost is childcare, which fades as children grow up. That makes term life a natural fit: a term that runs until the youngest child reaches independence, or a little beyond. Some couples buy matching policies on both parents for simplicity. Others choose a separate, shorter term on the stay-at-home parent because the need drops faster.

A spouse rider on the working parent's policy is another option some insurers offer, but it usually provides a limited amount and may end if the main policy ends or the marriage does. A separate policy is typically more flexible and stays with the insured person.

Practical steps for the conversation at home

Talking points for couples

  • List every weekly task the stay-at-home parent handles, including the invisible ones.
  • Decide what the surviving parent would realistically do: keep working full time, cut hours, or take a break.
  • Price local childcare for each child's age.
  • Check whether either parent has cover through work and whether it would continue if they left.
  • Agree who the beneficiary is and whether a trust makes sense for the children.
  • Put a review date in the calendar for when the youngest starts school.

Do not forget the working parent

This article focuses on the parent at home, but the family usually needs cover on both. If the working parent died, the household would lose income; if the stay-at-home parent died, it would gain large new costs. Protecting only one of those risks leaves a gap. Running a DIME estimate for each parent, side by side, gives a much clearer picture.

Frequently asked questions

Can a stay-at-home parent get life insurance without an income?

Yes. Insurers commonly offer cover to non-earning spouses, often with a maximum linked to the working spouse's income or cover.

Is a spouse rider enough?

It can help, but it usually provides a smaller amount and depends on the main policy. A separate policy is more flexible.

How long should the term be?

Many families choose a term that lasts until the youngest child is independent, because childcare needs fade after that.

Should both parents have the same amount of cover?

Not necessarily. The amounts should reflect what each parent contributes and what it would cost to replace.
