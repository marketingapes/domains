The Life Insurance Medical Exam: What Happens and How to Prepare

By the Smart Life Insurance Quote team · Last updated 23 September 2026

Educational information, not professional advice. Policies and rules vary by insurer and state; check details with a licensed professional.

The words "medical exam" put a lot of people off applying for life insurance. In practice, the exam is usually short, happens at your home, office or a local clinic, and involves nothing more invasive than a routine check-up. Knowing what to expect, and how to prepare, can make it quicker and help your results reflect your real health.

Who does the exam and where

Most exams are carried out by a paramedical examiner: a trained professional working for a company the insurer hires. They are not your doctor and they do not give medical advice or results on the spot. The insurer pays for the exam. You can usually choose a time and place that suits you, including early mornings, which helps if you are asked to fast. Many exams take somewhere around half an hour, although it depends on what the insurer has asked for.

What typically happens

  1. Identity check. The examiner confirms who you are with photo identification.
  2. Health questions. They go through a questionnaire covering your medical history, medications, doctors, family history and lifestyle. Some of these repeat questions from your application.
  3. Measurements. Height, weight and often waist size.
  4. Blood pressure and pulse. Often taken more than once during the visit.
  5. Blood sample. A small draw, tested for things such as cholesterol, blood sugar markers, liver and kidney function and nicotine. Screening for certain infections may be included, with your written consent.
  6. Urine sample. Used to check kidney function, sugar, protein, nicotine and some drugs.
  7. Extras when required. For older applicants or larger amounts of cover, an insurer may request an ECG or other tests.

How to prepare in the days before

Preparation checklist

  • Follow any fasting instructions exactly. Insurers commonly ask for several hours without food; water is usually fine.
  • Drink water beforehand so the blood draw and urine sample are easier.
  • Avoid heavy, unusual exercise for a day or so, since it can temporarily affect some results.
  • Avoid alcohol for at least a day before, and go easy on salty food.
  • Skip caffeine and nicotine on the morning of the exam; both can raise blood pressure temporarily.
  • Get a normal night's sleep and schedule the visit at a relaxed time.
  • Have ready: photo ID, a list of medications with doses, and names and contact details for your doctors.
  • Wear a short-sleeved or loose top to make the blood pressure check and blood draw easier.

Preparation is about avoiding temporary distortions, not hiding anything. Honest answers matter: misstatements on an application can give the insurer grounds to contest a claim during the contestability period, which usually lasts two years from issue.

If you feel nervous about blood or needles

Tell the examiner at the start. They can often let you lie down for the draw, and they do this many times a day. If your blood pressure tends to read high in medical settings, mention that too, and ask whether they can take several readings. Sitting quietly for a few minutes before the reading, feet flat and not talking, helps.

What happens after the exam

The samples go to a lab, and the results are sent to the insurer's underwriters along with your application and other records they check. Underwriters may request records from your doctor, which can add time. When a decision is made, you will be offered a rating class that sets your premium, asked for more information, or in some cases declined. Many insurers will share the lab results with you, or with your doctor, if you ask. If a result surprises you, discuss it with your own doctor, both for your health and because a correction or explanation can sometimes be submitted to the insurer.

Can you skip the exam?

Often, yes. Many insurers now offer accelerated underwriting for some applicants, using data sources and questionnaires instead of a physical exam. There are also simplified issue policies with a health questionnaire but no exam, and guaranteed issue policies with no health questions at all, usually with small face amounts and a waiting period before the full benefit applies. No-exam routes can be faster, but for some people a fully underwritten policy with an exam can result in a better rating class. The best route depends on your age, health and the amount of cover.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a life insurance exam take?

Many take around 20 to 45 minutes, depending on what tests the insurer requests.

Do I have to pay for the exam?

Normally no. The insurer arranges and pays for the paramedical exam as part of the application.

Can I reschedule if I am unwell on the day?

Yes. It is usually better to reschedule if you have a cold, fever or have just had an unusual night, because some results can be affected.

Will the exam results be shared with my doctor?

Not automatically. Many insurers will send results to you or your doctor if you request them.
