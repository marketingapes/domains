When to replace your pillow: the fold test and other honest signs

By the Pillow Exchange team · Last updated 23 September 2026 · 5 min read

Pillows wear out slowly, which is exactly why most people keep them too long. You adapt to the gradual flattening night by night and never notice the moment it stopped doing its job. There is no fixed date on which a pillow expires, but there are clear signs — and a couple of quick tests you can do right now.

The fold test

This works best for fibre, down and feather pillows.

  1. Take the case off and lay the pillow flat.
  2. Fold it in half across the middle, like closing a book.
  3. Let go.

A pillow with life left in it springs back open, or at least unfolds most of the way. One that stays folded, or slowly slumps open like a tired sandwich, has lost much of its loft and support. For large or very full pillows, fold into thirds, or place a light object such as a shoe on top of the folded pillow and see whether it can push it off.

Foam and latex do not fold the same way, so check them differently: look for permanent dents where your head rests, cracks, crumbling edges, or a noticeable dip that does not recover after a few minutes.

Other signs it is time

Stains are not just cosmetic. Yellowing usually comes from sweat and skin oils soaking through the case, which is also what dust mites feed on. A pillow protector slows this down a lot.

Rough lifespans by fill

Lifespans depend on quality, how you sleep, how warm you run and how well you look after the pillow, so treat these as broad tendencies rather than rules:

  • Polyester fibre: tends to be the shortest-lived; many people notice flattening within one to two years.
  • Down and feather: can last several years with regular fluffing, washing and a protector.
  • Memory foam: often a few years before it softens noticeably or develops a permanent dent.
  • Latex: generally among the most durable fills if kept out of sunlight.
  • Buckwheat: hulls gradually break down into dust; topping up with fresh hulls extends the pillow's life.

A common general rule is to reassess pillows every year or two. That is not a reason to throw out a pillow that still passes the tests; it is a reminder to actually do the tests.

Making a pillow last longer

  • Use a zippered protector under the case and wash it regularly.
  • Fluff fibre and down pillows every morning to redistribute the fill.
  • Rotate and flip the pillow so you are not always compressing the same spot.
  • Wash washable pillows correctly and dry them fully. See our pillow washing guide.
  • Do not sit or lean on your sleeping pillow as a back cushion; that compresses it far more than your head does.
  • Air pillows in a well-ventilated room every so often.

What to do with an old pillow

Most councils and waste services do not accept pillows in household recycling, and charity shops usually cannot take used bedding for hygiene reasons. Check with your local authority, but some better options are:

  • Animal shelters. Some accept clean old pillows and bedding for animal beds. Call first.
  • Textile recycling. Some textile banks and retailer take-back schemes accept bedding.
  • Around the house. Floor cushions, a pet bed, stuffing for draught excluders, a kneeling pad for gardening, or padding when moving furniture.

When you buy a replacement, give it time. Our guide to trying a new pillow explains why the first few nights are not a fair verdict.

Frequently asked questions

Can I restore a flat pillow?

A good wash and thorough tumble dry with dryer balls can revive fibre and down pillows somewhat. Adjustable pillows can be topped up with extra fill. Once a pillow fails the fold test after washing, restoring it is rarely worth it.

Is it unhygienic to keep a pillow for many years?

Over time pillows accumulate skin cells, sweat and dust mites. Protectors and regular washing help a great deal. If a pillow is stained, smelly or seems to worsen allergies, replace it.

Should I replace my partner's pillow at the same time?

Only if theirs is also worn. Different sleepers need different pillows, and there is no need to buy matching ones.

Good to know: this is general information about pillows and sleep habits, not medical advice. Ongoing pain, numbness, snoring with pauses in breathing, or sleep problems that last for weeks are worth raising with a doctor or physiotherapist.
