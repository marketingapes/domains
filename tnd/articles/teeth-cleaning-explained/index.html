Professional Teeth Cleaning Explained: Scaling, Polishing and Deep Cleans

By the The Nearest Dentists team · Last updated September 23, 2026
Educational information only. This is not dental or medical advice. Ask a licensed dentist about your own situation.

Even careful brushers build up deposits that a toothbrush cannot remove. A professional cleaning resets the surfaces of your teeth so your gums can stay healthy and your dentist can see clearly what is going on. The word "cleaning" covers a few different procedures, though, and it helps to know which one you are getting.

Plaque versus tartar

Plaque is the soft, sticky film of bacteria that forms on teeth every day. You can remove it with brushing and cleaning between your teeth. When plaque is left in place, minerals from saliva harden it into tartar, also called calculus. Tartar is rough and firmly attached, and it tends to form where saliva pools, such as behind the lower front teeth and on the cheek side of the upper molars. Once it has formed, you cannot brush it off. Its rough surface also collects more plaque, which irritates the gums.

That is the core job of a professional cleaning: removing tartar and plaque from places you cannot reach well, especially just below the gumline.

What happens during a routine cleaning

A routine cleaning is often called a prophylaxis, or "prophy," in dental offices. It is designed for mouths where the gums are healthy or only mildly inflamed. The typical steps are:

  1. Scaling. The hygienist removes tartar with hand instruments (scalers and curettes), an ultrasonic scaler that vibrates and sprays water, or both. The ultrasonic tool makes a high-pitched sound and a fine mist; it is loud but usually not painful.
  2. Polishing. A slow-turning rubber cup with a gritty paste smooths the tooth surfaces and removes surface stains. Some offices use an air-polishing device that sprays a fine powder with water instead.
  3. Flossing. The hygienist flosses between each tooth to clear leftover paste and debris, and to spot areas that bleed.
  4. Fluoride, if recommended. A varnish or gel may be applied to help strengthen enamel, particularly for people with a history of cavities, exposed roots or dry mouth.

Afterwards your teeth feel smooth, and you may notice slight tenderness in the gums for a day, especially if there was a lot of tartar.

Deep cleaning: scaling and root planing

When gum disease has progressed to periodontitis, the gums pull away from the teeth and form deeper pockets. Tartar and bacteria settle on the root surfaces inside those pockets, well below where a routine cleaning reaches. The treatment for this is called scaling and root planing, often shortened to SRP or described as a deep cleaning.

During SRP, the area is usually numbed with local anaesthetic, and the hygienist or dentist carefully removes deposits from the root surfaces and smooths them so the gums have a better chance to reattach and tighten. It is often done one quarter or one half of the mouth at a time, over two or more visits. The dentist may re-measure pocket depths several weeks later to see how the gums responded.

Why a deep clean might be recommended

Being told you need a deep cleaning can feel like a sales pitch, especially if it costs more than you expected or your insurance treats it differently from a routine cleaning. It is reasonable to ask for the evidence. The recommendation is normally based on pocket depth measurements, bleeding when probed, and bone loss visible on X-rays. Ask to see your pocket chart and the relevant X-rays, and ask how the numbers compare to healthy readings. If the explanation is clear and the measurements support it, SRP is a well-established treatment that can slow or halt gum disease. If not, a second opinion is perfectly reasonable.

After SRP, many people are placed on "periodontal maintenance" visits, which are more thorough than a routine cleaning and often scheduled more frequently. This is because gum disease is a chronic condition that tends to return without close monitoring.

Does it hurt?

Most routine cleanings are mildly uncomfortable at worst. Sensitive spots are common where gums have receded and roots are exposed. Tell the hygienist if an area is sore; they can adjust pressure, switch instruments, use a numbing gel, or in some cases numb the area with an injection. People with a lot of inflammation often find cleanings more comfortable over time as their gums get healthier, because healthy gums bleed and ache less.

Keeping it clean between visits

A cleaning is a reset, not a shield. What you do each day decides how much tartar builds up before your next appointment:

  • Brush twice a day for about two minutes with a fluoride toothpaste, angling bristles toward the gumline.
  • Clean between your teeth once a day with floss, floss picks, interdental brushes or a water flosser.
  • Limit how often you snack on sugary or starchy foods and sip sweet or acidic drinks.
  • Replace your toothbrush or brush head every three to four months, or sooner if bristles splay.
  • If you smoke or vape, ask about support to quit; tobacco is strongly linked with gum disease.

How often you need professional cleanings depends on how quickly you build tartar and on the health of your gums. Some people do well on twice-yearly visits; others benefit from more frequent appointments. Your hygienist can tell you which group you are in and why.

Frequently asked questions

Can I remove tartar at home?

No. Once plaque hardens into tartar it has to be removed with professional instruments. Home scraping tools can damage enamel and gums.

Is a deep cleaning the same as a regular cleaning?

No. Scaling and root planing treats gum disease by cleaning root surfaces inside deeper gum pockets, usually with numbing, and often over more than one visit.

Why do my teeth feel sensitive after a cleaning?

Removing tartar can expose root surfaces that were covered. Sensitivity usually fades within days; a sensitivity toothpaste can help. Mention persistent pain to your dentist.

Does whitening happen during a cleaning?

Polishing removes some surface stains, but it does not change the natural colour of your teeth the way whitening treatments do.
