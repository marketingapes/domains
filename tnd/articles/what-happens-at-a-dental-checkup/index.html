What Happens at a Dental Checkup, Step by Step

By the The Nearest Dentists team · Last updated September 23, 2026
Educational information only. This is not dental or medical advice. Ask a licensed dentist about your own situation.

A dental checkup can feel like a blur of bright lights, pokes and numbers called out across the room. Knowing what each step is for makes the visit less mysterious and helps you ask better questions. Practices vary in order and style, but most routine checkups for adults include the same core pieces. Here is what typically happens, and why.

Before you sit down: history and updates

Expect a health questionnaire at your first visit and a quick update at later ones. It asks about medical conditions, medications, allergies, pregnancy, smoking or vaping, and past dental problems. This is not paperwork for its own sake. Many medicines cause dry mouth, which raises cavity risk. Blood thinners affect bleeding during treatment. Some heart conditions and joint replacements may change whether antibiotics are considered before certain procedures. Diabetes is closely linked with gum disease. Be complete and honest; the team can only plan around what they know.

This is also the moment to mention anything that has been bothering you: sensitivity to cold, a chipped edge, bleeding when you floss, a clicking jaw, grinding at night. Write it down beforehand so you do not forget once you are in the chair.

X-rays, when they are due

Not every checkup includes X-rays. Your dentist decides how often you need them based on your age, history and risk of decay or gum disease. Bitewing X-rays show the areas between back teeth where cavities like to hide. Periapical X-rays show a whole tooth down to the root tip. A panoramic image shows the entire jaw on one film, which is useful for wisdom teeth and overall bone health. Modern digital X-rays use a low dose of radiation, and you can always ask why a particular image is recommended.

The cleaning

A dental hygienist usually performs the cleaning, although in some practices the dentist does it. They use hand scalers or an ultrasonic scaler to remove plaque and tartar (hardened plaque that brushing cannot shift), especially around the gumline and between teeth. Then they polish the teeth with a gritty paste and often floss for you. If you are prone to cavities, they may apply fluoride varnish, a sticky coating that is painted on and wears off over the next several hours. Our separate guide to professional cleanings covers this step in more detail.

Gum measurements: those numbers being called out

You may hear the hygienist reading out a string of numbers such as "3, 2, 3... 4, 3, 5." They are measuring the depth of the small groove between each tooth and the gum using a thin probe marked in millimetres. Healthy gums typically measure in the low single digits and do not bleed much when probed. Deeper readings, bleeding and gum recession can be signs of gum disease, which is common, often painless and much easier to manage when caught early. Ask what your numbers mean; they are a useful benchmark to track over time.

The dentist's exam

The dentist then examines each tooth with a mirror and a light, sometimes using a pointed instrument called an explorer to feel for soft spots. They check existing fillings and crowns for wear, cracks or gaps at the edges. They look at how your teeth meet when you bite, and for signs of grinding such as flattened edges. Many dentists also gently feel the jaw joints and the muscles of the face and neck.

An oral cancer screening is usually part of a routine exam, even if nobody announces it. The dentist looks at your tongue, the floor of your mouth, cheeks, palate and throat, and may feel the neck for swollen glands. Most findings are harmless, but a sore or patch that has not healed within about two weeks deserves a closer look.

The conversation at the end

The last few minutes are the most important for you. The dentist should summarise what they found and what, if anything, they recommend. A good summary covers three things: what is healthy, what needs watching, and what needs treatment now. If treatment is suggested, ask:

  • Can you show me on the X-ray or photo?
  • What happens if I wait, and how long is it reasonable to wait?
  • Are there other options, and how do they compare in lifespan and cost?
  • Can I have a written plan with an estimate and a pre-treatment check with my insurer?

You should also leave knowing when to come back. Many adults are seen every six months, but the right interval depends on your risk. People with gum disease may be seen more often; people with consistently healthy mouths are sometimes seen less often. Ask what interval your dentist suggests and why.

How to make your next checkup easier

Bring your insurance card and a list of current medications. Brush and floss beforehand as normal; there is no need to overdo it the night before, and sore gums from a sudden flossing spree can mislead the exam. If you feel nervous, say so at the start, agree on a hand signal for "please pause," and ask the hygienist to talk you through each step. Checkups are designed to find small problems while they are still small, and each visit is easier when you know what is coming.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a routine dental checkup take?

A routine visit with a cleaning commonly takes somewhere between 30 and 60 minutes. A first visit at a new practice often takes longer because of X-rays and a full exam.

Do I need X-rays at every checkup?

Not usually. Your dentist sets the interval based on your age, history and risk. You can always ask why a particular X-ray is recommended.

What do the numbers the hygienist calls out mean?

They are gum pocket depths in millimetres. Lower numbers with little bleeding generally indicate healthier gums; ask your hygienist to explain your readings.

Is it normal for gums to bleed during a cleaning?

Some bleeding is common, especially if you do not floss regularly, but frequent bleeding can be a sign of gum inflammation that is worth discussing.
