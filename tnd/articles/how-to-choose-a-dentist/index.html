How to Choose a Dentist You Will Actually Keep Seeing

By the The Nearest Dentists team · Last updated September 23, 2026
Educational information only. This is not dental or medical advice. Ask a licensed dentist about your own situation.

Most people pick a dentist the way they pick a lunch spot: whatever is closest and has a free slot. That works until you need a filling on a Friday afternoon, discover the practice does not take your plan, or realise you dread every visit because nobody explains anything. A little homework up front can save years of friction. This guide walks through a simple process for finding a general dentist who suits you, plus the questions worth asking before you book your first appointment.

Start with what you actually need

Before you search, jot down the handful of things that matter most. A general dentist (sometimes called a family dentist) handles checkups, cleanings, X-rays, fillings, many crowns and often simpler root canals and extractions. They refer out to specialists such as orthodontists, endodontists (root canal specialists), periodontists (gum specialists), oral surgeons and pediatric dentists when a case calls for it. For most adults and many families, a general dentist is the right front door.

Think about who in your household needs care. Some general practices happily see toddlers; others prefer children to see a pediatric dentist. If you have a specific worry such as missing teeth, gum disease, strong dental anxiety or a medical condition that affects treatment, note it now, because it changes which practices make your shortlist.

Build a shortlist of three to five practices

Good sources for names include your insurer's in-network directory (if you have dental coverage), recommendations from friends or coworkers who have similar needs, your physician, and your state dental association or dental board website. Map search results are handy for seeing what is nearby, but treat star ratings as a starting point rather than a verdict. Read the text of recent reviews and look for patterns: repeated comments about surprise bills, rushed appointments or excellent explanations tell you more than an average score.

Distance matters more than people expect. A practice ten minutes from home or work is one you will actually return to twice a year. Check parking or transit, and whether the building is accessible if anyone in your family uses a wheelchair or stroller.

Check the basics before you book

Licence. Every US state has a dental board that licenses dentists, and most boards offer an online lookup that shows whether a licence is active and whether there are public disciplinary actions. It takes two minutes and is worth doing for anyone you plan to trust with your mouth.

Hours. If you work standard office hours, ask about early mornings, evenings or Saturdays. A practice that is only open while you are at work will quietly turn into a practice you never visit.

Emergency policy. Ask what the office does when an existing patient breaks a tooth or develops swelling. Some keep same-day slots, some have an after-hours phone line, some share on-call duty with nearby practices. The answer tells you a lot about how the office treats patients once they are on the books.

Costs and coverage. Dental fees vary between practices and regions, and they depend on the exact procedure and materials. A good office will tell you whether they are contracted with your plan and will provide a written estimate before larger treatment. If you do not have insurance, ask whether they offer an in-house membership plan or payment arrangements; many do.

What to notice at the first visit

The first appointment is a two-way interview. You are there for a checkup, but you are also learning how the practice works. Pay attention to a few signals:

  • Explanations. Does the dentist show you what they see, on an X-ray, an intraoral camera image or a hand mirror? You should leave understanding the state of your teeth and gums in plain language.
  • Options. For anything beyond routine care, you should hear about more than one approach where more than one reasonable approach exists, including the option of waiting and monitoring when that is appropriate.
  • Pace. Feeling hurried through a big treatment decision is a warning sign. It is normal to ask for time to think, a printed plan or a second opinion.
  • Infection control. Gloves, masks, sealed instrument pouches and wiped-down surfaces should be routine, not something you need to ask about.
  • Front desk. The people who answer the phone handle your scheduling, claims and questions. Friendly, organised staff make everything easier.

Red flags worth taking seriously

A few situations justify pausing and getting another opinion. Be cautious if you are told you need a large amount of work but the dentist cannot show you why, if you are pressured to sign up for financing on the spot, or if a practice promises that a costly cosmetic treatment will fix problems it is not designed to fix. Be equally cautious if a practice refuses to share your X-rays or records when you ask. Your records belong to your care, and you can generally request copies to take to another provider, sometimes for a modest copying fee.

A second opinion is not an insult. Dentists often disagree on timing, especially for things like small cavities, old fillings and cosmetic work. If two careful dentists recommend the same plan, you can proceed with more confidence.

Switching dentists without losing your history

If you decide to move on, ask the old practice to send your recent X-rays, charting and treatment notes to the new one. Doing this avoids unnecessary repeat X-rays and gives your new dentist a baseline to compare against. Settle any outstanding balance first so records transfer smoothly, and tell the new office about any treatment that was planned but not finished.

Special situations

Tight budget. Dental school clinics, where students treat patients under faculty supervision, often charge lower fees in exchange for longer appointments. Community health centers may offer sliding-scale fees based on income. Your local health department can usually point you to options.

Children. Pediatric dentists complete extra training in treating children and children with special health care needs. Many families use them until the teen years, then move to a general dentist.

Medical conditions. If you take blood thinners, have a heart condition, diabetes, are pregnant or are having cancer treatment, look for a dentist who asks detailed health questions and is comfortable coordinating with your doctor.

Choosing well once means fewer decisions later. Spend an hour on the shortlist and the phone calls, trust what you notice at the first visit, and you are far more likely to end up with a dentist you will still be seeing years from now.

Frequently asked questions

Should I choose a general dentist or a specialist?

Start with a general dentist for routine care and most common treatments. They will refer you to a specialist such as an endodontist, periodontist or oral surgeon when your situation calls for it.

How can I check whether a dentist is licensed?

Use your state dental board's online licence lookup. It usually shows licence status and any public disciplinary actions.

Is it rude to ask for a second opinion?

No. Asking for a second opinion on a large or non-urgent treatment plan is common, and careful dentists expect it.

Can I get my X-rays sent to a new dentist?

Generally yes. Ask your previous office to forward recent X-rays and records; some offices charge a small copying fee.
