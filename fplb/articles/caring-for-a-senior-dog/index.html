Senior pets

Caring for a Senior Dog: Comfort, Mobility and Everyday Joy

By the For Pets Like Blue team · Updated September 23, 2026 · 4 min read

Talk to your vet. This guide is general information for healthy pets, not veterinary advice. Every animal is different — if you are worried about your pet’s health, or anything here does not match what you are seeing, contact your vet.

Older dogs have a particular sweetness. They know the routine, they know you, and they are often content with simple pleasures: a sunny patch, a slow sniff around the block, a nap nearby. Ageing also brings changes in joints, senses, weight and sometimes memory. A few adjustments at home and a good partnership with your vet can make the senior years comfortable and full of good days.

When is a dog “senior”?

There is no single birthday. Large and giant breeds tend to age faster than small breeds, so a giant breed might be considered senior at around six or seven while a small dog might not show signs of ageing until well past that. Many vets suggest more frequent check-ups — often twice a year — once a dog reaches their senior years, because problems caught early are often easier to manage.

Make the home easier to navigate

Stiff joints and weaker muscles make everyday obstacles harder. Look around your home from the perspective of an older dog.

  • Non-slip rugs or runners over slippery wooden or tiled floors, especially on regular routes
  • A supportive, thick bed with low sides that is easy to step into, placed away from draughts
  • Ramps or steps for getting into the car or onto a favourite sofa
  • Baby gates at the top of stairs if balance or eyesight is failing
  • Raised food and water bowls if bending down seems uncomfortable
  • Night lights in hallways for dogs with poorer vision
  • Toe grips or dog boots for traction, if your vet agrees they are suitable
  • Keeping furniture in the same places, which helps dogs with reduced sight

Exercise: shorter, gentler, more often

Older dogs still need exercise to maintain muscle, joint mobility and a healthy weight, but the shape of it often changes. Two or three shorter walks usually suit a senior dog better than one long one. Let them set the pace and give plenty of time for sniffing, which is gentle on joints and good for the brain. Swimming or hydrotherapy may be options for some dogs; ask your vet first.

Watch for signs that a walk was too much: stiffness later that day or the next morning, lagging behind or lying down during the walk. Adjust the length accordingly.

Keep the mind busy too

Mental activity is just as important as physical exercise. Simple food puzzles, scatter feeding, gentle scent games and short training sessions for new tricks all help keep older minds engaged. Yes, you can teach an old dog new tricks — just keep sessions brief and use soft, tasty treats if teeth are sensitive.

Some older dogs develop canine cognitive dysfunction, a condition with similarities to dementia. Signs can include getting lost in familiar places, staring at walls, changes in sleep patterns such as pacing at night, forgetting house training, or changes in how they interact with the family. Tell your vet if you notice these; there are management strategies that may help.

Recognising pain

Dogs are good at hiding discomfort, and many owners put slowing down down to “just old age” when pain is a factor. Signs that may point to pain include:

Possible signs of pain in older dogs
What you might seeWhy it matters
Reluctance to jump, climb stairs or get upCommon with joint problems such as arthritis
Stiffness after resting, which eases with movementA typical pattern with arthritis
Licking or chewing at a joint or pawMay indicate localised pain

Never give your dog human pain medication. Common human painkillers such as ibuprofen and paracetamol (acetaminophen) can be toxic to dogs. Your vet can prescribe medicines designed for dogs and discuss other supports.

Food, weight and water

Many older dogs become less active and gain weight easily, which puts extra strain on joints. Others lose weight or muscle. Weigh your dog regularly and talk to your vet about whether a diet change is appropriate. Keep an eye on water intake as well: drinking and urinating noticeably more than usual can be a sign of several conditions and should be checked.

Dental health matters at every age, and sore teeth can make an older dog reluctant to eat. Gentle toothbrushing and regular dental checks help.

Enjoy the everyday

Senior dogs thrive on routine and companionship. Keep days predictable, include them in family life, and look for new ways to have fun — slow car rides to a new sniffing spot, a gentle game of hide-the-treat, or simply sitting in the garden together.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a senior dog see the vet?

Many vets recommend check-ups about every six months for older dogs, but your vet will suggest a schedule based on your dog’s health.

Can I give my old dog joint supplements?

Some owners use joint supplements, but evidence varies by product and ingredient. Ask your vet before starting any supplement, especially if your dog takes other medication.

My older dog seems confused at night. What can I do?

Tell your vet, as night-time restlessness can be linked to pain, other illness or cognitive changes. Meanwhile, keep a consistent routine, use a night light and make sure your dog has a toilet break just before bed.
