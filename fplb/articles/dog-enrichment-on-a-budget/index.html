Dog Enrichment on a Budget: 15 Games From Things You Already Own

By the For Pets Like Blue team · Updated September 23, 2026 · 4 min read

Enrichment simply means giving a dog chances to do the things dogs are built to do: sniff, search, chew, lick, dig, shred, chase and solve problems. A dog who gets to use their nose and brain every day is often calmer at home, and a ten-minute sniffing game can take the edge off a rainy day when a long walk is not possible. The good news is you do not need a basket of expensive gadgets. Most of the best games use things already in your kitchen or recycling bin.

Why sniffing and problem-solving matter

A dog’s sense of smell is extraordinarily powerful, and using it takes real concentration. That is why a slow, sniffy walk can leave a dog more settled than a fast march around the same route. Food puzzles and searching games tap into the same drive to forage. They also build confidence: a shy dog who learns that effort leads to a reward often becomes a little bolder in everyday life.

Start easy. A puzzle that is too hard leads to frustration or giving up. Make the first version almost impossible to fail, then make it harder in small steps.

Sniffing and searching games

  1. Find it scatter. Toss part of a meal across the lawn or a rug and say “find it”. Simple and very effective.
  2. Towel snuffle. Lay an old towel flat, sprinkle kibble over it, then scrunch it loosely. Tighter scrunching makes it harder.
  3. Towel roll-up. Scatter treats along a towel and roll it up like a sausage. The dog nudges it open to find the treats.
  4. Cup shuffle. Put a treat under one of three upturned plastic cups. Start with the treat visible, then let the dog pick with their nose.
  5. Hide-and-seek treats. Ask the dog to wait in another room (or have someone hold them) while you hide treats at nose height around a room, then release them to search.

DIY food puzzles

  1. Muffin-tin puzzle. Drop treats into some cups of a muffin tin and cover every cup with a tennis ball or a small toy.
  2. Bottle spinner. An empty plastic bottle with the cap, label and ring removed and a few dry treats inside. Supervise closely and bin it once it cracks.
  3. Egg-box surprise. Put treats in a cardboard egg box, close it and let the dog open it. Remove any cardboard they try to eat.
  4. Toilet-roll parcels. Fold the ends of a cardboard tube over a few treats for the dog to tear open.
  5. Frozen lick mat or stuffed toy. Spread or stuff plain, dog-safe food and freeze it for a longer-lasting challenge.

Shredding, chewing and thinking games

  1. Box of boxes. Fill a larger cardboard box with smaller boxes, scrunched paper and a few treats. Great for dogs who love to rip things up. Remove tape and staples first.
  2. Name the toy. Teach your dog the name of one toy by saying it as you play, then ask them to fetch it from a choice of two.
  3. Hand target games. Teach a nose touch to your palm, then ask for touches in different places around you.
  4. Which hand? Hide a treat in one closed fist and let the dog choose. Reward the correct nose-bump.
  5. Sniffari walk. A slow walk where your dog picks the direction and sniffs as long as they like.

Safety check for DIY enrichment

Home-made games are great, but a few rules keep them safe:

  • Supervise any game involving cardboard, paper or plastic, and take it away once the treats are found
  • Remove tape, staples, plastic windows and labels before offering boxes and tubes
  • Avoid toxic foods: chocolate, grapes and raisins, onions and garlic, macadamia nuts, alcohol, and anything containing xylitol (a sweetener found in some peanut butters and sugar-free products)
  • Count enrichment food as part of the daily ration so your dog does not gain weight
  • Separate dogs during food games if either guards food or toys
  • Choose a puzzle size that cannot be swallowed whole

Building a weekly enrichment routine

You do not need to do everything every day. A simple plan might be: feed one meal a day from a puzzle or scatter, do one five-minute brain game, and give one slow, sniffy walk a few times a week. Rotate games so they stay interesting. If your dog loses interest, make the game easier or use a more exciting treat for a while.

Watch your dog’s body language. Wagging, loose movement and focus mean the game is working. Barking, pawing frantically or walking away can mean it is too hard or too exciting. Adjust and try again.

Frequently asked questions

How much enrichment does a dog need each day?

There is no fixed number. Many dogs do well with a combination of daily walks with sniff time, at least one meal fed through a puzzle or scatter, and a short training or brain game. Adjust for age, breed and energy.

Is peanut butter safe for dogs?

Plain peanut butter without xylitol is commonly used in small amounts, but always read the label. Xylitol is very dangerous for dogs. Peanut butter is also high in fat and calories, so use it sparingly.

My dog destroys puzzle toys in seconds. What can I do?

Use sturdier options such as frozen stuffed rubber toys, feeding scatters, or sniffing games that do not rely on an object. Always supervise so pieces are not swallowed.
