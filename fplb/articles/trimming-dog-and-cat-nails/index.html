Grooming

Trimming Dog and Cat Nails Without the Drama

By the For Pets Like Blue team · Updated September 23, 2026 · 4 min read

Talk to your vet. This guide is general information for healthy pets, not veterinary advice. Every animal is different — if you are worried about your pet’s health, or anything here does not match what you are seeing, contact your vet.

Nail trims are one of those jobs many pet owners dread — and many pets agree. But overgrown nails are more than cosmetic. Long dog nails change the way a dog stands and walks, can split or break, and in extreme cases curl into the paw pad. Cats who cannot keep their claws in check may get them stuck in carpet or overgrown into the pad, which is especially common in older cats. With patience and the right technique, most pets can learn to accept regular trims.

Know the anatomy: the quick

Inside each nail is the quick, which carries blood vessels and nerves. Cutting into it hurts and bleeds, and one bad experience can make a pet wary of nail trims for a long time. In pale nails you can usually see the quick as a pink area inside. In dark nails it is hidden, so you trim a tiny amount at a time and look at the cut surface: when a darker, moist-looking dot starts to appear in the centre, you are close and should stop.

The quick grows longer as nails grow. Regular small trims encourage it to recede over time, which is why little and often works better than occasional big trims.

Building comfort with handling first

Before you trim anything, teach your pet that paw handling predicts good things. Over several days:

  1. Touch a paw briefly, then give a treat.
  2. Hold the paw for a second or two, then treat.
  3. Gently press a toe, then treat.
  4. Show the clippers, touch them to a nail without cutting, then treat.
  5. Make the clipping sound near the paw (clip a piece of dry spaghetti), then treat.

Only move on when your pet is relaxed at the current step. A lick mat smeared with something tasty can keep a dog busy during early sessions.

How to trim a dog’s nails

  • Choose a quiet time and good light; sit on the floor with a small dog or have a larger dog lie on their side
  • Hold the paw firmly but gently, pressing lightly on the toe to extend the nail
  • Cut at a slight angle, following the curve of the nail, taking off only the tip
  • For dark nails, take off thin slices and check the cut surface after each one
  • Do not forget dewclaws on the inside of the leg, which do not touch the ground and can overgrow
  • Stop after one or two nails if your dog is getting worried — finish another day

A quick rule of thumb many people use: if you can hear your dog’s nails clicking on a hard floor, they are probably due for a trim.

How to trim a cat’s claws

Cats’ claws are retractable. Hold your cat gently on your lap facing away from you, take one paw and press lightly on the top and bottom of a toe to extend the claw. Trim just the sharp, clear tip, well away from the pink quick. Many cats tolerate only a few claws per session, and that is fine. Try a relaxed moment, such as when your cat is drowsy after a meal.

Cats who scratch regularly on a good post often need less frequent trims, although senior cats may need more help because their claws can thicken and they may scratch less.

If you cut the quick

It happens, even to professionals. Stay calm, press a pinch of styptic powder onto the nail tip for a short while, and the bleeding usually stops. Cornflour can be used if you have nothing else. Offer a treat and end the session on a positive note. If bleeding does not stop within several minutes, or the toe looks swollen or sore in the following days, call your vet.

If your pet becomes very distressed, do not force it. Groomers and vet practices can trim nails, and your vet can talk to you about options for anxious pets.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I trim my dog’s nails?

It depends on how fast they grow and how much time your dog spends on hard surfaces. Many dogs need a trim every few weeks. Small, frequent trims are easier and help the quick recede.

Should I declaw my cat to stop scratching?

Declawing is an amputation of the last bone of each toe and is banned or restricted in many countries and some US states. Scratching is normal behaviour. Regular trims, good scratching posts and soft nail caps are humane alternatives; talk to your vet if scratching is a problem.

Is a grinder better than clippers?

Neither is better for every pet. Grinders make it easier to shorten dark nails gradually and leave smooth edges, while clippers are quicker and quieter. Some owners clip first and then smooth with a grinder.
