Loose-Leash Walking: Teach Your Dog to Stop Pulling

By the For Pets Like Blue team · Updated September 23, 2026 · 4 min read

Dogs pull for a simple reason: it works. Every time a dog leans into the leash and gets closer to the lamppost, the other dog or the park, pulling pays off. Dogs also naturally walk faster than people, and the world is full of fascinating smells. Loose-leash walking is not about forcing a dog to march beside your knee; it is about teaching them that a slack leash is the way to get where they want to go.

What “loose leash” actually means

A loose leash hangs in a relaxed “J” shape between you. The dog can be a little in front, a little behind or off to one side — they just are not putting tension on the leash. This is different from a formal “heel”, where the dog stays in a set position. For most everyday walks, a loose leash is all you need, and it leaves the dog free to sniff and enjoy themselves.

Gear that makes it easier

Equipment will not train your dog for you, but the right setup helps.

  • A standard leash of around 1.5–2 m (5–6 ft). Retractable leashes teach dogs that pulling extends their range, which works against this training.
  • A well-fitted harness. A front-clip harness, where the leash attaches at the chest, can reduce pulling power by turning the dog gently to the side. Make sure it does not rub behind the front legs.
  • A treat pouch with small, soft, high-value treats you can deliver quickly.

Avoid equipment designed to cause pain or discomfort. It can create fear or frustration around walks, and reward-based methods are effective without it.

Step-by-step: building the skill

Start somewhere boring — your hallway, kitchen or garden — before you try it on a real walk.

  1. Reward position. With the dog on leash, stand still. Each time the dog is near your side, drop a treat by your leg. You are teaching that the space next to you is a great place to be.
  2. Add a step. Take one step. If the dog stays with you and the leash is slack, reward at your leg. Then two steps, then three.
  3. Increase the pace of rewards, not the distance. Early on, reward every few steps. As the dog gets it, spread the rewards out gradually.
  4. Change direction. Turn around cheerfully and reward the dog for following. Frequent turns keep the dog tuned in to you.
  5. Move to the street. Start with quiet times and routes, and go back to rewarding very frequently.

When the leash goes tight: “be a tree”

The moment the leash tightens, stop and stand still. Do not jerk the leash or pull back. Wait. Most dogs will eventually look back or step towards you, which puts slack in the leash. The instant that happens, praise and move forward again. The lesson is clear: pulling stops the walk, a loose leash makes it continue.

Alternatively, try “reverse direction”: when the dog pulls, cheerfully turn and walk the other way, rewarding when they catch up with a loose leash. Some dogs learn faster with this because it keeps things moving.

Both methods need consistency. If pulling works some of the time, the dog will keep trying it. In the early weeks, it may help to do separate “training walks” and use a different setup, such as a harness with a back clip, for walks where you simply cannot stop, so the rule stays clear.

Sniff breaks are part of the deal

Sniffing is how dogs read the news. Rather than banning it, use it as a reward. Walk a few metres on a loose leash, then say a release cue like “go sniff” and let the dog explore. Real-world rewards like sniffing, greeting a friend or heading into the park are powerful motivators once your dog understands the rules.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to teach loose-leash walking?

It varies a lot with the dog’s age, history and how consistent the training is. Many people see a real improvement within a few weeks of short daily practice, but expect to keep rewarding occasionally for a long time.

Is a harness or a collar better for a dog that pulls?

A well-fitted harness spreads pressure across the chest instead of the neck, which many people prefer for dogs that pull. A front-clip harness can also reduce pulling while you train. Ask your vet if your dog has neck, back or breathing issues.

Can older dogs learn to stop pulling?

Yes. Dogs of any age can learn loose-leash walking with consistent, reward-based practice. It may take a little longer to undo a long-standing habit, but the method is the same.
