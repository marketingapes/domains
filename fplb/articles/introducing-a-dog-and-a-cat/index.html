Introducing a Dog and a Cat: A Slow, Safe Plan

By the For Pets Like Blue team · Updated September 23, 2026 · 4 min read

Plenty of dogs and cats live together happily, and some become genuine friends. But their first meeting sets the tone. A dog who chases a cat on day one may learn that chasing is great fun, and a cat who is cornered may decide the dog is a permanent threat. A slow introduction, spread over days or weeks, gives both animals time to get used to each other without fear. The steps below work whether you are bringing a new dog to a resident cat or the other way around.

Before they meet: set up the home

Give each animal their own area to begin with. The cat needs a room the dog cannot enter, with litter box, food, water, scratching post and hiding spots. Throughout the house, the cat should also have escape routes and high places — shelves, cat trees, the top of a wardrobe — where the dog cannot follow.

  • Baby gates with a cat-sized gap or a cat flap, so the cat can move between rooms but the dog cannot
  • Litter box somewhere the dog cannot reach (many dogs will raid it)
  • Cat food placed up high or in a dog-free room
  • High-value treats for both animals
  • A leash and harness for the dog during early meetings

The staged introduction plan

Move to the next stage only when both animals are calm at the current one. Some pairs get through the stages in a week; others need a month or more.

Staged dog and cat introduction
StageWhat to doReady to move on when
1. Scent swapSwap bedding or rub a soft cloth on each animal’s cheeks and leave it near the other’s foodBoth sniff the item calmly and eat normally
2. Door diningFeed each pet on opposite sides of a closed door, starting far away and moving closerBoth eat relaxed with bowls near the door
3. Sight through a gateShort sessions with the dog leashed behind a baby gate and the cat free to come and goDog can look away from the cat and take treats; cat is not hiding
4. Same room, dog leashedDog on a loose leash, cat free with high escape routes; reward calm behaviourDog settles on a mat; cat moves around normally
5. Supervised freedomLeash dragging or removed, short sessions, you actively supervisingRepeated calm sessions with no chasing

Keep early sessions short — a few minutes is plenty — and end them before anyone gets stressed. Separate the animals whenever you are not home to supervise, possibly for several weeks.

Teaching the dog what to do

It helps to give the dog a job. Before introductions start, practise a “look at me” cue, a “leave it”, and settling on a mat, all with great rewards. During sessions, reward the dog every time they glance at the cat and then look back at you, or choose to lie down calmly. You are teaching them that ignoring the cat is the best way to get good things.

Never let the dog chase the cat, even in play. Chasing is self-rewarding for many dogs and the habit can escalate quickly. If the dog fixates, stares intently or lunges, calmly move them further away and make the next session easier.

Reading body language

Watch both animals closely. Good signs include loose, wiggly body language in the dog, sniffing then turning away, and a cat who walks around with tail up or sits calmly watching. Warning signs include:

  • Dog: hard staring, a stiff and still body, trembling with excitement, whining, lunging or ignoring food and cues.
  • Cat: flattened ears, puffed tail, hissing, growling, crouching low, hiding for long periods, or refusing to eat or use the litter box.

If you see these signs, go back a stage. Pushing through stress rarely leads to friendship.

Special situations

Puppies and kittens often adapt more easily, but a boisterous puppy can still frighten an adult cat. Supervise closely and give the cat plenty of high escape routes.

Dogs with a strong chase drive, such as many sighthounds and terriers, may never be fully safe loose with cats. That does not mean living together is impossible, but management — gates, separate rooms, supervision — may always be needed.

Older or arthritic cats may struggle to jump to high escape routes, so add steps and ground-level hiding boxes.

If after several weeks you are making little progress, or there is any aggression, a qualified behaviourist can assess your household and build a tailored plan. Rescue organisations can often tell you whether an adopted dog has been “cat tested”, which is a useful starting point but not a guarantee.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take a dog and cat to get along?

It can take anywhere from a few days to several months. Let the animals’ body language, not a calendar, decide when to move to the next step.

Should I let them “sort it out” themselves?

No. Unsupervised first meetings can lead to injuries and lasting fear. A gradual, supervised introduction is much safer.

My cat hisses at the new dog. Is that bad?

Hissing is a normal warning that the cat wants more space. Increase the distance, give the cat escape routes and slow down the introduction. Hissing often reduces as the cat learns the dog is not a threat.
