Travel

Road Trips With Pets: Packing, Safety and Rest Stops

By the For Pets Like Blue team · Updated September 23, 2026 · 4 min read

Talk to your vet. This guide is general information for healthy pets, not veterinary advice. Every animal is different — if you are worried about your pet’s health, or anything here does not match what you are seeing, contact your vet.

Travelling with a pet can turn a holiday into a proper family trip, but a long drive is a lot to ask of an animal who usually only rides to the vet. With some preparation, most dogs and many cats can learn to travel calmly. This guide covers getting your pet used to the car, keeping them secure while you drive, and planning stops that keep everyone comfortable.

Practise before the big trip

If your pet associates the car only with the vet or kennels, start weeks before you travel. Build it in stages:

  1. Sit in the parked car with your pet for a few minutes, with treats or a chew, then get out.
  2. Turn the engine on while sitting still. Reward calm behaviour.
  3. Drive to the end of the road and back.
  4. Take short trips to somewhere fun, like a park or a friend’s garden.
  5. Gradually build to journeys of an hour or more.

For cats, leave the carrier out at home with the door open and a soft blanket inside so it becomes a normal resting place rather than a sign of trouble.

Keep your pet secure in the car

An unrestrained pet can distract the driver and can be thrown forward in a sudden stop. In some places, laws or highway codes also require animals to be suitably restrained. Options include:

  • A secured crate or carrier, strapped in or wedged so it cannot slide. For cats, a carrier is the safest choice.
  • A car harness attached to a seat-belt connector. Look for harnesses that have been independently crash-tested, and attach them to the harness, never a collar.
  • A dog guard or barrier for dogs travelling in the boot of an estate or SUV.

Keep pets out of the front seat, where airbags can cause serious injury. However tempting it looks, do not let your dog ride with their head out of the window: flying debris can injure eyes and ears.

Road-trip packing checklist

  • Collar with an up-to-date ID tag, plus a harness and a spare leash
  • Microchip details updated with your mobile number
  • Food for the whole trip plus a day or two extra, in the brand they usually eat
  • Bottled or home tap water and a collapsible bowl
  • Medications, with copies of prescriptions and vaccination records
  • Poo bags, paper towels and enzyme cleaner
  • Portable litter tray and litter for cats
  • Familiar bedding and a favourite toy
  • A basic pet first-aid kit
  • Details of vets near your route and destination, including an emergency practice
  • A recent photo of your pet in case they get lost

Planning stops and the day’s rhythm

Give your pet a good walk or play session before you set off, and feed a light meal a few hours before departure rather than just before, which can help with car sickness. Many people plan a stop roughly every two to three hours for dogs to stretch, drink and toilet. Always clip the leash on before opening the car door — rest stops near busy roads are no place for a dog to bolt.

Cats usually stay in their carrier for the whole journey. On very long drives you can offer water and a litter tray inside the closed car at a stop, but be very careful: a frightened cat can escape through an opened door in a second.

Heat: never leave a pet in a parked car

Cars heat up very quickly, even on days that do not feel especially hot, and cracking a window does little to help. Dogs and cats can develop heatstroke in a short time. Plan stops so someone can stay with the pet, or choose places where pets are welcome inside. Use sunshades on rear windows and keep the air conditioning on while driving.

Car sickness and anxiety

Drooling, lip-licking, yawning, whining and vomiting are signs your pet is struggling. Facing forward, a well-ventilated car, and a lighter meal before travel help some pets, as does gradual practice. Puppies often grow out of car sickness. For pets who still struggle, speak to your vet before the trip; they can advise on anti-nausea or anti-anxiety options that are safe for your pet. Never give your pet human travel-sickness medicines unless your vet has told you to.

At your destination

Confirm pet policies with hotels or rentals before you book, including any size limits or fees. When you arrive, check the space for hazards such as open balconies, gaps in fences, toxic plants or accessible medicines. Keep the routine as normal as you can, and do not leave your pet alone in an unfamiliar place until they have settled.

Frequently asked questions

How long can a dog travel in a car without a break?

Many people stop every two to three hours so dogs can stretch, drink and toilet. Puppies, older dogs and dogs with health conditions may need more frequent stops.

Should I sedate my pet for a road trip?

Only on your vet’s advice. Some sedatives can affect balance and temperature control. Your vet can suggest options suited to your pet’s health and the journey.

Can my cat travel loose in the car?

It is much safer to keep cats in a secured carrier. A loose cat can get under the pedals, distract the driver or escape when a door is opened.
