Indoor Cat Enrichment: Play Like a Hunter

By the For Pets Like Blue team · Updated September 23, 2026 · 4 min read

Indoor cats can live long, safe and happy lives, but they rely on us to supply the excitement that the outdoors would otherwise provide. A bored cat may sleep more than usual, overeat, pester you at night, or find their own entertainment by ambushing ankles and shredding sofas. The fix is not complicated: give your cat regular chances to stalk, pounce, climb, scratch and explore.

Play like prey: the hunting sequence

Cats are hunters, and the most satisfying play copies a hunt. Think of it as a sequence: stalk, chase, pounce, catch, then eat, groom and sleep. When play stops before the “catch”, some cats stay wound up. When it ends with a catch and a snack, it tends to settle them.

Two short sessions a day of about 10–15 minutes suit many cats, though kittens and young cats often want more and older cats less. Evening sessions, before the last meal, are a good way to take the edge off night-time zoomies.

How to use a wand toy properly

A wand toy is the single most useful piece of kit for indoor play, but technique matters.

  1. Move the toy like prey. Prey moves away from a cat, not towards it. Drag the toy across the floor, around a chair leg, behind a cushion.
  2. Mix speed and stillness. Hide the toy, pause, then twitch it. The stillness makes the pounce more thrilling.
  3. Use cover. Cats like to stalk from behind something. A box or a blanket fold on the floor gives them an ambush spot.
  4. Let them win. Allow regular catches. Let the cat hold, bite and bunny-kick the toy for a moment.
  5. Wind down. Slow the movements for the last minute, let the cat make a final catch, then offer a treat or meal.

Watch for signs of tiredness such as lying down, disinterest or panting. Cats are sprinters, and panting during play is a signal to stop and let them rest. If your cat pants easily, mention it to your vet.

A note on laser pointers and string

Laser pointers can be fun, but a cat can never catch the dot. If you use one, finish by landing the dot on a real toy or treat the cat can grab. Never shine the beam in a cat’s eyes. String, ribbon, hair ties and wand-toy cords are dangerous if swallowed, so put wand toys away out of reach after each session.

Climbing, perching and scratching

Cats think in three dimensions. Height gives them a place to watch, rest and escape from children, dogs or other cats. You can add vertical territory without a big budget:

  • A sturdy cat tree or a series of shelves that let the cat climb to a high resting spot
  • A window perch or cleared sill with a view of birds, trees or the street
  • At least one tall, stable scratching post and one horizontal scratcher — cats have different preferences
  • Scratchers placed near sleeping spots and at room entrances, where cats like to mark territory
  • Cardboard boxes and paper bags (with handles removed) for hiding and ambushing
  • Resting spots in quiet places as well as in the middle of family life

Food puzzles and foraging

In the wild, cats eat many small meals and work for each one. Food puzzles bring back some of that effort. Start very simply so your cat succeeds straight away, then make it harder:

Beginner to advanced cat food puzzles
LevelPuzzle idea
BeginnerTreats in an open egg box or on a flat tray with raised edges
BeginnerA short trail of dry food leading up a cat tree
IntermediateToilet-roll tubes glued upright in a shallow box, with kibble inside to paw out
IntermediateA plastic bottle with holes cut slightly larger than the kibble (smooth the edges)
AdvancedRolling treat balls or commercial puzzle feeders with sliding lids

Count puzzle food as part of the daily food allowance. Cats who are overweight can benefit from this extra activity, but ask your vet before cutting their food.

Rotating toys and adding novelty

Many cats lose interest in toys that are always lying around. Keep a small “toy library” in a closed box and rotate a few out each week. New smells can be enriching too: some cats react strongly to catnip or silvervine, while others ignore them entirely. A cardboard box that arrived with the shopping may be the best toy of the week.

Finally, consider safe outdoor experiences if they suit your cat and home. Some people use secure window boxes or enclosed patios so their cats can enjoy fresh air and outdoor sights without roaming.

Frequently asked questions

How do I stop my cat attacking my ankles?

Ankle ambushes usually mean pent-up hunting energy. Schedule regular wand-toy sessions, especially before the times the ambushes happen, and never use your hands or feet as toys. Redirect to a thrown toy if an ambush is about to happen.

Why does my cat ignore expensive toys and play with boxes?

Boxes offer cover, novelty and a place to hide and pounce from, which is exactly what a hunter wants. Toys usually come alive only when a person moves them like prey.

Is it cruel to keep a cat indoors?

Indoor cats can live full, healthy lives when their environment gives them things to do: climbing, scratching, hunting play, window views and company. Many welfare organisations support indoor living with good enrichment.
