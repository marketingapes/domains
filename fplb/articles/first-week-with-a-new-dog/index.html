Your New Dog’s First Week: A Calm, Day-by-Day Plan

By the For Pets Like Blue team · Updated September 23, 2026 · 6 min read

Bringing a dog home is exciting for you and, very often, overwhelming for them. Everything smells new, the people are strangers and the rules are unknown. The first week is not the time to teach a long list of tricks or introduce the dog to every friend and neighbour. It is the time to make the world predictable. A dog who knows when food arrives, where the toilet spot is and where they can rest undisturbed settles faster and learns more easily later.

This plan works for puppies and adult dogs, including rescues. Adjust the pace to the dog in front of you: a confident young dog may be exploring happily by day three, while a shy adult may need the whole week just to eat comfortably in front of you.

Before the dog arrives: set up a base camp

Pick one quiet area as the dog’s home base for the first few days — a corner of the kitchen, a spare room or a pen in the living room. It should be away from the front door and the busiest foot traffic, and easy to clean.

  • A bed or crate with soft bedding, placed against a wall rather than in the middle of the room
  • Water bowl and food bowl on an easy-wipe floor
  • The same food the shelter, breeder or previous owner was using, if you can get it
  • A flat collar or harness with an ID tag showing your phone number
  • A standard fixed-length leash (around 1.8 m / 6 ft is typical)
  • Two or three safe chew items and a simple food puzzle
  • Baby gates or closed doors to block off stairs and rooms you are not ready to share
  • Enzyme cleaner for accidents — ordinary cleaners can leave a scent that invites repeat visits

Walk through the space at dog height. Cables, shoes, bin lids, houseplants and anything on a low coffee table are fair game for a curious or anxious new arrival. Moving them now is easier than training around them later.

Days 1–3: decompress and observe

Many rescue organisations talk about a rough “3-3-3” pattern: about three days to decompress, three weeks to learn the routine, and three months to feel fully at home. It is a guideline, not a rule, but it is a helpful reminder that the first days are mostly about rest.

On the first day, take the dog straight to the toilet spot on leash before going inside. Then let them explore the base camp at their own speed. Resist the urge to follow them around or pick them up constantly. Sit nearby, talk softly, and let them come to you.

Keep visitors away for now. Keep walks short and in quiet places — around the block or even just the garden is plenty. A new dog may not have a reliable recall, may slip a loose collar, and may not yet know your voice, so everything outside stays on leash.

Some dogs do not eat much in the first day or so, and loose stools from stress and a change of scene are common. Offer meals in a quiet spot, pick the bowl up after 15–20 minutes, and try again at the next mealtime. If your dog refuses food for more than a day, has repeated vomiting or diarrhoea, or seems lethargic, call your vet rather than waiting.

Days 4–7: build a simple routine

Dogs relax when the day has a shape. Write a basic timetable and stick to it as closely as you can for the rest of the week.

TimeWhat happensWhy it helps
Wake-upStraight outside to the toilet spot, then breakfastSets up toilet success first thing
MorningShort sniffy walk, then a rest in base campSniffing tires the brain without over-exciting

Build in short practice absences, even if you work from home. Step out of the room for a minute, then five, then fifteen, while the dog has a chew. Coming and going without fuss teaches the dog that being alone is ordinary and that you always come back.

Toilet training from day one

Assume every new dog, even an adult who was “house trained” somewhere else, needs a refresher in a new home. Take them out after waking, after eating, after play, and at regular intervals in between — every hour or two for puppies, every few hours for adults. Go to the same spot, stand quietly, and praise and reward calmly the moment they finish.

If an accident happens indoors, clean it up without comment. Scolding tends to teach a dog to hide when toileting, which makes training harder. Instead, ask yourself what you missed: too long between trips, a signal like sniffing and circling, or too much freedom too soon.

First vet visit and paperwork

Book a check-up within the first week or two, and bring any records you received. Your vet can confirm vaccination and parasite-prevention status, check the microchip scans correctly, and talk you through a feeding plan. Update the microchip registration with your own contact details — a chip is only useful if it points to the right person.

If you want to change food, do it gradually over roughly a week by mixing increasing amounts of the new food into the old. Sudden switches are a common cause of upset stomachs.

Gentle training that fits the first week

Keep training sessions tiny and fun. Good first-week skills are name recognition (say the name, reward the look), a hand target, “sit” for meals, and settling on a mat. Reward-based methods using food, toys and praise are widely recommended because they build trust as well as behaviour.

Save bigger adventures — busy parks, dog-friendly cafés, long car journeys, meeting other dogs — for when your dog is clearly relaxed at home. There is no deadline. A dog who feels safe will be far more ready for the wider world in a month’s time than one who was rushed in week one.

Frequently asked questions

Should my new dog sleep in my bedroom?

Either choice can work. Many people find a crate or bed in or near the bedroom helps a new dog settle and makes night-time toilet trips easier. Whatever you choose, keep it consistent from the first night so the dog is not learning one rule and then another.

How soon can I let my new dog off leash?

Wait until you have a reliable recall in low-distraction places and the dog is clearly bonded to you, which usually takes weeks, not days. Use a long line in safe open areas to practise before true off-leash time, and follow local leash laws.

My new dog is hiding and will not come to me. What should I do?

Give them space and time. Sit quietly in the same room, toss the odd treat nearby without asking for anything, and let them approach on their terms. Most shy dogs come out of their shell gradually as the routine becomes predictable.

When should I introduce my new dog to other dogs?

Once your dog is settled at home, start with one calm, known dog on neutral ground, walking side by side rather than face to face. Avoid dog parks until you know how your dog handles greetings.
