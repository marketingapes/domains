How to Spot a Real Deal (and a Dressed-Up Regular Price)

By The Discount Deal Me team · Last updated September 23, 2026 · 4 min read

A discount is only as honest as the number it is measured against. "40% off" sounds precise, but it tells you nothing until you know what the 40% is taken from, and whether anybody ever actually paid that starting price. Most of the skill of deal hunting is learning to look past the percentage and ask a few boring questions about the baseline, the total and the timing.

This guide walks through those questions in the order we find easiest to use while you are standing in a checkout flow with a timer ticking down in the corner.

Start with the reference price, not the percentage

Every sale tag has two numbers: the price you pay and a reference price it is compared against. Retailers label that reference in different ways, such as "was", "compare at", "list price", "MSRP" or "regular price", and those labels are not interchangeable. A "was" price suggests the same seller charged it recently. "Compare at" often points to what other sellers supposedly charge. MSRP is the manufacturer's suggested price, which many products rarely sell at for long.

In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission's long-standing guides on deceptive pricing say a former-price comparison should be based on a price the item was actually offered at, in good faith, for a reasonably substantial period. Many states have their own rules too. That does not mean every reference price you see is reliable; it means a vague or inflated one is a reason to slow down, not a reason to buy faster.

A useful habit: mentally delete the reference price and look only at the number you would pay. Then ask whether that number is good compared with what the item usually costs, which is a different question from whether it is lower than the tag says.

Five checks that take under two minutes

You do not need special software to sanity-check most offers. The table below lists the quick checks we lean on, what each one tells you, and the warning sign to watch for.

Red flags that deserve a second look

Some patterns show up again and again on offers that are less generous than they look. None of them proves a deal is bad, but each is a cue to run the checks above.

  • Permanent urgency. A countdown timer that resets when you reload the page, or a "sale ends tonight" banner that has been there all month.
  • Round-number reference prices. A "was" price that sits suspiciously above every other seller you can find for the identical item.
  • Bundles you did not ask for. "Save 30% on the set" where you only wanted one piece and the single item is not discounted at all.
  • Percentages without prices. "Up to 70% off" usually means a handful of items reach 70% and most get far less.
  • Pressure copy about stock. "Only 2 left!" can be true, but it is also an easy line to add to a page.
  • Coupon sites that want your login. A real promo code never needs your email password or bank details to reveal it.

Worked example: comparing two "deals"

Here is an illustrative example with made-up round numbers, not a real product. Store A shows a blender "was $120, now $84, 30% off". Store B shows the same model at $89 with no sale badge at all. Store A charges $9.99 shipping under a $99 order minimum; Store B ships free. Store A's sale is final; Store B gives 30 days to return.

On the headline alone, Store A looks like the win. On total cost, Store A is $93.99 and Store B is $89. On flexibility, Store B also lets you change your mind. The "30% off" badge was measuring against a reference price that did not matter to your decision. This is the most common way a real-looking deal turns out to be the more expensive choice.

When a real discount still is not a deal for you

A price can be genuinely low and still be a poor buy. If you would not have bought the item at its usual price in the next few months, the "saving" is money spent rather than money kept. If the cheaper version is noticeably lower quality and you will replace it sooner, the per-year cost may be higher. And if a deal pushes you to buy three of something perishable, you are only saving if you use all three.

A simple test: write down what you would pay today for this exact item if it were not on sale. If the sale price is below that number and the item was already on your list, it is a deal. If the number you wrote is zero, it is a purchase you did not plan.

Frequently asked questions

Is MSRP a fair reference price?

Sometimes, but not reliably. Many products routinely sell below the manufacturer's suggested price, so a discount measured from MSRP can overstate the saving. Compare against what the item actually sells for.

How can I tell what an item usually costs?

Use a price-history tool if one covers the store, check the same model at two or three other sellers, or keep a simple note of prices you see for things you buy often.

Are countdown timers always fake?

No. Many are tied to real promotion end dates. The issue is that a timer is not evidence the price is good, so treat it as a reminder to check, not a reason to skip checking.

What is the single most useful check?

Total cost for the identical item, including shipping, fees and any required add-ons, compared with at least one other seller.
