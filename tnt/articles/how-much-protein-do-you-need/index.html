Nutrition & meal prep

How Much Protein Do You Actually Need? A Plain-English Guide

By the Toned ’N Tasty team•Last updated September 23, 2026

Protein advice online ranges from "everyone eats too much" to "you need a shake every two hours". The truth is calmer than either. There are well-established baseline guidelines, a sensible range that many sports nutrition organisations suggest for people who train, and a lot of room for personal preference in how you get there. This guide walks through the numbers, what they are based on, and how to translate them into meals.

Educational information, not medical advice. Protein needs vary with age, health and activity. If you have kidney disease, diabetes, are pregnant, or have any medical condition, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing your diet.

The baseline: the RDA

In the United States, the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for protein for healthy adults is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. For someone weighing 70 kg (about 154 lb), that works out to about 56 grams a day. The RDA is designed as the amount that meets the basic needs of nearly all healthy adults; it is a floor to avoid deficiency, not a target for building muscle or performing at your best.

To convert pounds to kilograms, divide by 2.2. A 180 lb person weighs about 82 kg, so the RDA would be roughly 65 grams per day.

What people who train often aim for

People who do regular resistance training commonly aim higher than the RDA. The International Society of Sports Nutrition, for example, has published a position stand suggesting that about 1.4 to 2.0 grams per kilogram per day is enough for most exercising people to build and maintain muscle. Other expert groups give similar ranges. Needs toward the higher end are often discussed for people who are in a calorie deficit and trying to preserve muscle, and some research suggests older adults may benefit from more protein than the RDA as well.

These are general reference points, not a prescription. If you carry a lot of body fat, some professionals calculate needs using a goal or adjusted body weight instead, which gives a lower number. That is one of many reasons personal advice from a professional beats a formula.

Spreading protein across the day

Total daily intake matters most. Beyond that, many people find it easier to reach their number, and feel fuller, when protein is spread over meals rather than crammed into dinner. A common practical approach is to include a solid source of protein at each meal, often somewhere around 20 to 40 grams, with a protein-containing snack if needed.

Here is what that could look like for someone aiming at roughly 120 grams:

  • Breakfast (about 30 g): Greek yogurt overnight oats, or three egg muffin cups with toast.
  • Lunch (about 35 g): Leftover chili, or a big salad with a can of tuna and chickpeas.
  • Snack (about 15 g): Cottage cheese with fruit, or a glass of milk and a handful of nuts.
  • Dinner (about 40 g): A palm-and-a-half of salmon or chicken with rice and vegetables.

Food first, supplements if convenient

Whole foods bring protein plus fibre, vitamins and minerals, and they tend to be more filling. That said, protein powders are simply food in a convenient form. If a shake helps you reach your target on busy days, that is a reasonable choice. Look for products that have been independently tested by a third-party certification program, because dietary supplements are not reviewed for effectiveness before they are sold, and check the ingredient list for anything you want to avoid.

Plant-based eaters can meet protein needs too. Beans, lentils, tofu, tempeh, edamame, seitan and soy milk are all useful. Eating a variety of plant proteins over the day helps cover the full range of essential amino acids.

Signs you might be under-eating protein (and what to do)

There isn't a reliable at-home test, but some practical clues are worth noticing: you are hungry soon after meals, your meals are mostly refined carbohydrates, or you are training hard and not seeing any progress in strength over several months. None of these prove anything on their own. The practical step is simply to look at what you eat on a typical day, estimate the protein using food labels, and see how it compares with the ranges above. If you are consistently far below, add one protein-rich food to each meal and reassess in a few weeks.

Can you eat too much protein?

For healthy adults, intakes within the ranges above are widely considered safe. Very high intakes are unnecessary and can crowd out other foods, like vegetables, fruit and whole grains, that you also need. People with existing kidney disease often need to limit protein and should follow their medical team's advice. If you are unsure, a registered dietitian can give you a number that fits your health, your goals and the way you like to eat.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 1 gram per pound rule accurate?

One gram per pound of body weight is about 2.2 g/kg, slightly above the common 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg range for people who train. It is an easy-to-remember upper estimate rather than a requirement.

Do I need protein right after a workout?

Total daily protein matters more than precise timing. Having a meal with protein within a few hours of training is a sensible, easy habit.

Does cooking reduce protein?

Cooking does not meaningfully destroy protein. Weights change because food loses or gains water, which is why cooked and raw values per 100 g differ.

Should I track protein every day?

Not necessarily. Tracking for a week or two can teach you what normal portions provide; after that, many people rely on a protein-at-every-meal habit.
