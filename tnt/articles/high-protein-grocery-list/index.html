The High-Protein Grocery List: What to Buy and How Much Protein It Has

By the Toned ’N Tasty team•Last updated September 23, 2026

Eating more protein gets much easier when the right foods are already in your kitchen. This list is organised by the way most grocery stores are laid out, so you can shop in one pass. It includes animal and plant options, quick-cooking and shelf-stable picks, and approximate protein numbers so you can see how everyday portions add up.

How to read the numbers

The protein figures below are rounded, approximate values for common portions, based on typical nutrition label and database figures. Brands, cuts and preparation methods differ, so treat these as ballpark numbers and check the labels on what you actually buy. Weights for meat and fish are for cooked portions unless noted, because that is what ends up on your plate.

Produce section

Produce isn't where most protein comes from, but it is what makes high-protein meals taste good and keeps them balanced. Stock up on sturdy vegetables that last all week: bell peppers, onions, carrots, cabbage, broccoli and sweet potatoes. Add fast-cooking greens like baby spinach for eggs and bowls, plus lemons and limes, because a squeeze of citrus rescues almost any meal-prep container on day three. Edamame, peas and fresh herbs are worth picking up too.

Meat, fish and the deli counter

  • Chicken thighs and breasts: Family packs are often cheaper per pound. Portion and freeze what you won't cook in a day or two.
  • Lean ground turkey or beef: The base for chili, tacos, pasta sauce and lettuce wraps.
  • Frozen fish fillets and shrimp: Found in the freezer aisle, often better value than the fresh counter, and they cook in minutes.
  • Rotisserie chicken: Not the cheapest per gram of protein, but a huge time-saver. Shred it the day you buy it.

Dairy and eggs

This aisle is one of the most efficient places to shop for protein. Eggs are versatile and quick. Plain Greek yogurt and Icelandic-style skyr are thick, tangy and work in sweet or savoury dishes; buying the large tubs and adding your own fruit keeps sugar and cost down. Cottage cheese is great on toast, blended into eggs, or eaten with fruit. Milk and high-protein ultrafiltered milk are easy additions to oats and smoothies. If you avoid dairy, soy milk is the plant milk closest to dairy milk in protein; many almond and oat milks contain very little, so check labels.

Pantry and canned goods

The pantry is the budget hero. Canned beans, lentils and chickpeas are inexpensive, last for a long time, and add protein and fibre to chili, salads, soups and bowls. Canned tuna, salmon and sardines make a no-cook lunch in two minutes. Dried lentils cook in about 20 minutes without soaking. Peanut butter and other nut butters add some protein, although they are mostly fat, so they work best as a flavour booster rather than the main protein. Whole grains like quinoa, oats, whole-wheat pasta and farro contribute more protein than you might expect, too.

Freezer aisle

Frozen vegetables are picked and frozen at their peak and are just as useful as fresh for cooked dishes. Frozen edamame, peas, stir-fry vegetable mixes, fish fillets, shrimp and chicken all belong on a meal-prepper's list. Frozen fruit is ideal for overnight oats and smoothies.

Printable shopping checklist

  • Eggs (1–2 dozen) and a tub of plain Greek yogurt
  • Cottage cheese and milk (or soy milk)
  • Chicken thighs or breasts, and 1–2 lb lean ground turkey
  • Frozen salmon or shrimp
  • Canned beans (2–3 cans), canned tuna (2 cans), dried lentils
  • Extra-firm tofu or tempeh
  • Frozen edamame and frozen berries
  • Rolled oats, rice or quinoa, whole-grain tortillas
  • Peppers, onions, broccoli, spinach, cucumbers, lemons and limes
  • Salsa, soy sauce, spices, and something you genuinely enjoy

Budget tips

Compare the unit price on the shelf tag (price per ounce or per pound) rather than the package price. Store brands of basics like beans, oats, frozen vegetables, yogurt and eggs are usually a good deal. Buy meat in larger packs when it is on sale, then portion and freeze it flat in labelled bags. Build a few meals a week around beans and lentils, which are among the least expensive protein sources in the store. And plan meals before you shop, so nothing you buy gets forgotten at the back of the fridge.

Frequently asked questions

Why do cooked and raw protein numbers differ?

Meat loses water when it cooks, so the same piece weighs less afterwards. The protein stays, which means cooked meat has more protein per gram than raw.

Is canned fish healthy?

Canned tuna, salmon and sardines are convenient protein sources. If you eat a lot of tuna, or are pregnant, follow current guidance from your health authority on mercury and choose lower-mercury options.

Are high-protein snack bars worth it?

They can be convenient, but they vary widely in sugar and cost. Plain yogurt, eggs, and cottage cheese usually give you more protein for less money.

What is the cheapest protein?

It depends on local prices, but dried beans and lentils, eggs, and store-brand canned beans are often among the least expensive per gram of protein.
