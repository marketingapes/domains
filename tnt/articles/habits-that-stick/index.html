Habits

How to Build Eating and Training Habits That Actually Stick

By the Toned ’N Tasty team•Last updated September 23, 2026

Most people know roughly what they should do: eat more vegetables and protein, move more, sleep better. The hard part is doing it on a random Tuesday when work ran late and the couch is calling. That gap between knowing and doing is where habits live. Motivation comes and goes, so the goal is to make healthy behaviour the easy, automatic option, even on low-motivation days.

Start smaller than feels useful

The most common mistake is starting too big: five gym sessions a week, a full diet overhaul and a 6 a.m. alarm, all from Monday. It feels exciting for a week and exhausting by the second. A better approach is to shrink the habit until it seems almost too easy to skip. Instead of "work out for an hour", start with "put on my gym clothes and do one set of squats". Instead of "meal prep every week", start with "cook one extra portion of dinner for tomorrow's lunch".

This works because consistency builds the habit, and the habit makes it easier to add more later. A two-minute version done every day is more valuable at the start than an ambitious plan done twice.

Anchor habits to things you already do

Behaviour researchers have long studied what are called implementation intentions, plans in the form "When X happens, I will do Y". Having a specific when and where makes you much more likely to follow through than a vague goal. A simple version is to attach a new habit to something already in your day, sometimes called habit stacking.

Design your environment

Your surroundings nudge you all day. You can use this in your favour by making good choices visible and easy, and less helpful choices a little less convenient. None of this relies on willpower; it relies on friction.

  • Put a fruit bowl on the counter and keep cut vegetables at eye level in the fridge.
  • Store treats out of sight, in an opaque container, or simply buy them in single portions.
  • Leave your gym bag packed by the door, or your trainers next to the bed.
  • Keep a water bottle on your desk.
  • Pre-portion snacks into small containers when you unpack the shopping.
  • Schedule workouts in your calendar like any other appointment.

Track the behaviour, not just the result

Body weight and body measurements move slowly and bounce around from day to day, which can be discouraging. Tracking the behaviours you control is more motivating: workouts completed, days you ate a protein-rich breakfast, nights you were in bed by eleven. A paper calendar where you tick off each day gives you a visible chain that you won't want to break. Many people find that a simple checkmark is surprisingly satisfying.

Keep the tracking light. If logging takes longer than a minute a day, it tends to become another habit that doesn't stick.

Plan for bad days

You will miss days. Everyone does, because life includes sick kids, deadlines and holidays. What matters is what happens next. A useful rule is "never miss twice": missing one workout or one planned meal is just a blip, but two in a row starts to become a new pattern. When you miss, make the next action as small as possible to get back on track.

It also helps to decide in advance what your "minimum" version looks like. On a chaotic day, the full workout might become ten minutes of bodyweight squats, push-ups and a walk. Dinner might be eggs on toast with a bag of salad instead of the planned recipe. The minimum keeps the habit alive.

Make it enjoyable

You are much more likely to keep doing things you enjoy. That sounds obvious, but many plans are built around foods people dislike and workouts they dread. Choose recipes you look forward to, like the ones on this site. Pick a form of movement you like, whether that is lifting, dancing, hiking or a sport. Pair workouts with a favourite podcast, or train with a friend. Enjoyment isn't a luxury; it is a strategy.

A four-week starter plan

  1. Week 1: Pick one habit only. Make it tiny and anchor it to an existing routine.
  2. Week 2: Keep the first habit. Adjust your environment to make it easier.
  3. Week 3: If week two felt easy, grow the habit slightly, for example from one set to a full short workout.
  4. Week 4: Review your tracker. If the habit feels mostly automatic, add a second one.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to form a habit?

It varies a lot by person and by habit. Simple habits can feel automatic within weeks, while bigger changes take longer. Focus on consistency rather than a countdown.

What if I lose motivation?

Expect it. Rely on cues, a tiny minimum version and your environment rather than motivation, and return to the plan the next day.

Should I change my diet and exercise at the same time?

You can, but many people do better changing one thing at a time. Add the second habit once the first feels easy.

Do rewards help?

Small, immediate rewards, such as ticking a box or saving a favourite podcast for workouts, can make a new habit feel more satisfying.
