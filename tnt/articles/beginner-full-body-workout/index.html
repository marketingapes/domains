A Beginner's Full-Body Strength Workout, Three Days a Week

By the Toned ’N Tasty team•Last updated September 23, 2026

If you are new to strength training, the best program is one that is simple enough to follow, trains your whole body, and lets you add a little more over time. Full-body workouts, done three times a week on non-consecutive days, check every box. This plan uses two alternating workouts, A and B, built around basic movement patterns you will use for years.

Check with a professional first if needed. This is general fitness information, not medical advice. If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, a recent injury, are pregnant, or haven't exercised in a long time, check with your doctor before starting. Stop if you feel sharp pain, dizziness or chest discomfort.

Why full-body, three days a week?

Beginners don't need to train each muscle to exhaustion. Early on, strength improves quickly, partly because your nervous system is learning the movements. Practicing each movement pattern two or three times a week gives you frequent practice without much soreness. Three sessions also leave rest days between them, which is when your body recovers and adapts. A Monday, Wednesday and Friday schedule is classic, but any three non-consecutive days work.

The two workouts

Alternate the workouts: A, B, A in week one, then B, A, B in week two, and so on. Each workout should take about 45 minutes including the warm-up.

Rest about 60 to 120 seconds between sets of the bigger lifts and a little less for the core exercises. You can also pair exercises, for example a set of squats, a short rest, a set of push-ups, then back to squats, to save time.

How heavy should it be?

Choose a weight that lets you finish the target reps with good form while feeling like you could do two or three more. Trainers call this "reps in reserve". If you blast past the top of the rep range with lots left in the tank, the weight is too light; if your form breaks down before the bottom of the range, it is too heavy. Your first week is about learning the movements, so start conservatively. It is far better to finish feeling like you could have done more than to be too sore to come back on Wednesday.

Exercise swaps for home or gym

  • No dumbbells? Squat to a chair holding a backpack, do push-ups, use a loaded backpack for rows, and use resistance bands for pulldowns and presses.
  • Push-ups too hard? Put your hands on a kitchen counter or a sturdy bench. The higher your hands, the easier it gets. Lower the height over time.
  • Knees complain on split squats? Take a shorter step, hold on to something for balance, or swap in step-ups onto a low step.
  • At a gym? A leg press can replace goblet squats, a seated cable row can replace dumbbell rows, and a machine chest press can replace push-ups while you build strength.

Warm-up in five minutes

  1. Two to three minutes of easy movement: brisk walking, marching or cycling until you are slightly warm.
  2. Ten bodyweight squats, ten glute bridges and ten arm circles in each direction.
  3. One or two light "ramp-up" sets of your first exercise, using about half your working weight.

That's enough. Long static stretching before lifting isn't necessary for most people; save it for after the session if you enjoy it.

Form cues for the key lifts

Quick checklist

  • Goblet squat: hold the weight at your chest, feet about shoulder-width apart, sit down between your heels, keep your chest up, and push the floor away to stand.
  • Romanian deadlift: soft knees, push your hips back as the weights slide down your thighs, keep your back flat, and stop when you feel a stretch in your hamstrings.
  • Push-up: hands under your shoulders, body in a straight line from head to heels, elbows angled back slightly rather than flared straight out.
  • Row: brace a hand on a bench, pull the dumbbell towards your hip, and pause for a moment at the top.
  • Overhead press: squeeze your glutes, keep your ribs down and press up until your arms are straight beside your ears.

Filming a set on your phone and comparing it with a trusted video demonstration is one of the easiest ways to check your technique. If you can afford a few sessions with a qualified coach, it is money well spent at the start.

How long to run the program

Stick with this plan for at least 8 to 12 weeks, adding reps and then weight over time (our guide to progressive overload explains how). Keep a simple log of the weights and reps you do. When progress stalls for several weeks in a row despite good sleep and eating, that is the time to consider a more advanced program, not before.

Frequently asked questions

Will I get bulky lifting weights?

Building a lot of muscle takes years of focused training and eating. For most beginners, lifting simply makes them stronger and more capable, and changes how they look gradually.

Can I do cardio too?

Yes. Walking, cycling or other cardio on non-lifting days, or after a session, fits well alongside this plan. Start moderately so you can recover.

How sore should I be?

Some muscle soreness in the first couple of weeks is common. It usually fades as your body adapts. Sharp or joint pain is different, and a reason to stop and get it checked.

What if I miss a workout?

Just do the next workout in the sequence on your next available day. There is no need to double up.
