Why the failing lane failed

Not one rejection was on the merits.

This is the part most agencies never look at, because it requires the client's outcome data rather than the ad platform's. Here is every reason the firm gave for turning down an auto intake:

  • Not looking to hire a lawyer13
  • Client unresponsive12
  • Bad phone number5
  • Spam3
  • Replied STOP3
  • Signed elsewhere before contact1
  • Never requested contact1
No liability call. No damages call. No statute problem. No "already represented." Roughly two thirds is contactability and a third is wrong intent. The intake team never got far enough to evaluate a single claim. That is a routing and contact-rate problem — not a verdict on the market.

By contrast, the abuse lane's turn-downs were genuine screening: age at the time of the incident, location, whether the setting was institutional, statute of limitations. Those conversations actually happened. That is what a working lane looks like even when it says no.

The method

We optimize toward signed cases, because that is the only number that pays.

Cost per lead flatters everybody. It said the auto lane was the cheaper of the two. Cost per signed case said the auto lane was infinite, and it was right.

Getting to that number requires three things most engagements never assemble: media spend at campaign level, a stable identity that survives from ad click to intake record, and the client's own outcome export joined back against both. When those three line up you stop arguing about lead quality and start reading a scoreboard.

What we would do differently, stated before the money moves: stop funding the lane that signs nothing, move that budget to the lane already converting, and put a contact-rate fix in front of it. Same spend. On the measured rate, roughly ten more signed cases.
