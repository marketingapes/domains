Seasonal Cash Flow: How to Survive the Slow Months and Fund the Busy Ones

By the What Is Working Capital teamLast updated September 23, 20264 min read
Educational, not advice. Examples use made-up round numbers. Check decisions with an accountant or adviser who knows your business.

Seasonal businesses face a cruel timing problem. The months when they spend the most, buying stock, hiring and marketing, come just before the months when customers actually pay. A business can be profitable over the year and still run out of cash in the spring.

The good news is that seasonal cash flow is predictable. If you map it once, you can see the squeeze coming months ahead and arrange funding while you still look like a strong borrower.

Map the year month by month

Take last year's bank statements or accounting records and total the cash that actually came in and went out each month. Not sales and expenses as booked, but money in and money out, including loan repayments, tax payments and owner drawings. Then run a cumulative balance from your opening cash.

Here is an illustrative patio and garden furniture shop. It buys most of its stock from January to March, sells from April to August, and ticks over quietly in autumn and winter. It starts the year with $40,000 in the bank.

Over the year the shop generated $31,000 of net cash, ending at $71,000. By any annual measure it is doing fine. But at the end of March the bank balance would be $30,000 overdrawn, and within the month it could be worse, because stock invoices and payroll rarely land on the same day as customer receipts. That trough is the number to plan around.

Find the trough, then add a buffer

The lowest point of the cumulative line tells you roughly how much funding you need. Add a margin of safety for things going wrong: a cold, wet spring that delays sales by a month, a supplier asking for payment earlier, or a slightly larger order than planned. For the example shop, planning for a peak need of around $40,000 to $45,000, rather than exactly $30,000, would be prudent.

Once you know the shape, a rolling 13-week cash forecast lets you track the trough week by week as it approaches, rather than discovering it on a Friday morning.

Ways to fund the gap

There are two broad approaches: reduce the trough, and fund whatever remains.

Reduce the trough

  • Keep last summer's cash. The simplest seasonal funding is a reserve set aside at the peak. The example shop had $90,000 in September; ring-fencing $40,000 of it removes the need to borrow at all.
  • Stage your stock buying. Ordering in two or three waves, with the later ones based on early-season sales, spreads the cash out and reduces the risk of being left with unsold stock. The inventory guide covers reorder logic.
  • Ask for seasonal dating. In some industries suppliers let seasonal retailers take delivery early and pay once the season is under way. See supplier payment terms.
  • Take deposits or pre-orders for custom or large items in the quiet months.
  • Build off-season revenue. Storage, repairs, winter lines or services can soften the quiet months, though only if they cover their own costs.

Fund what is left

A revolving line of credit is the classic fit because you draw only what you need, when you need it, and repay as sales come in. Some lenders offer lines designed for seasonal businesses, and some require the balance to be paid down to zero for a period each year (often called a clean-up or rest period), which is easy for a genuinely seasonal business to meet. Apply after your strong season, when your accounts look best, not in February when the balance is already falling.

Avoid funding a predictable seasonal gap with expensive short-term products that take fixed daily repayments. Those repayments continue through the slow months too, and can make the next trough deeper. Our guide to the true cost of short-term financing shows how quickly the cost adds up.

A seasonal planning checklist

  • Build a 12-month cash map from last year's actual bank movements.
  • Identify the lowest cumulative balance and the month it occurs.
  • Add a buffer for a late or weak season.
  • Decide how much will come from reserves and how much from credit.
  • Arrange any credit facility after your peak, while the numbers are strong.
  • Set a rule for how much of peak-season cash is set aside for next year.
  • Review the map each year; seasons shift as a business grows.

Frequently asked questions

My business is only a little seasonal. Does this matter?

Even a mild pattern, such as a slow January or a summer dip for a B2B service, can combine with annual bills like insurance or tax to create a tight month. Mapping the year takes an hour and tells you whether you need to plan.

Should I lay staff off in the slow season?

That is a business and people decision beyond cash alone. Many seasonal firms use a small core team plus seasonal hires. Check employment rules where you operate before changing hours or contracts.

How big should my cash reserve be?

For a seasonal business, a good starting point is the size of your trough plus a buffer, as shown above. Non-seasonal businesses often think in months of fixed costs instead.
