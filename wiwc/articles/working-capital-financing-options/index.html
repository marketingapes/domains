Financing

Working Capital Financing Options Compared: Lines of Credit, Factoring, MCAs and More

By the What Is Working Capital teamLast updated September 23, 20265 min read
Educational, not advice. Examples use made-up round numbers. Check decisions with an accountant or adviser who knows your business.

When a business needs cash to cover the gap between paying out and getting paid, there is no shortage of offers. Search for "working capital" and nearly every result is a lender's page. This guide is the opposite: a plain comparison of the main options, what each one is actually designed for, and the questions worth asking before you commit.

Product names, rules and availability differ by country and lender. The descriptions here are general and use common US terminology; treat them as a map, not a recommendation.

Start with the matching principle

The single most useful idea in business financing is to match the funding to the need. Short-term, self-repaying needs, such as buying stock that will sell in eight weeks, suit short-term, flexible funding. Long-lasting needs, such as equipment or a permanently higher level of working capital after growth, suit longer-term funding. Mismatches cause trouble: using a short-term product to buy a machine means repaying it long before the machine has earned its keep.

So before comparing products, write down: what exactly the money is for, how and when it will come back (which customer payments or sales will repay it), and whether the need is one-off, seasonal or permanent. Our guides to seasonal cash flow and growth help with that.

Free or nearly free sources first

Before any lender, check the sources that cost little or nothing:

  • Your own cycle. Collecting receivables faster, trimming slow stock and paying suppliers on (not before) the due date can release more cash than a small loan would provide. See the cash conversion cycle.
  • Trade credit. Longer supplier terms are usually interest-free.
  • Customer deposits on custom or large orders.
  • Retained profit or owner equity, which carries no repayment schedule, though it does carry opportunity cost.

Security, guarantees and the fine print

Much small-business credit comes with strings that are easy to miss:

  • Personal guarantees. Many lenders ask owners to guarantee business debt personally, which puts personal assets at risk if the business cannot pay.
  • Liens. In the US, lenders commonly file a UCC lien, sometimes a "blanket" lien over all business assets, which can make it harder to borrow from anyone else.
  • Covenants. Promises such as keeping a minimum current ratio or providing quarterly accounts.
  • Automatic debits. Daily or weekly repayments taken straight from the account reduce flexibility in slow weeks.
  • Renewal and exit. What happens at the end of the term, how much notice is needed to leave, and whether there are early-repayment charges.

Questions to ask any lender

  • What is the total amount I will repay, in dollars, including every fee?
  • What is the annual percentage rate (APR) or equivalent annualised cost?
  • How often are repayments taken, and can they flex with my sales?
  • What security, liens or personal guarantees are required?
  • Are there penalties or no savings for repaying early?
  • What happens if I miss a payment?
  • Is this product regulated as a loan where I operate?

If a provider cannot or will not answer the first two questions clearly, that is a signal in itself. Our guide to the true cost of short-term financing shows how to convert factor rates and fees into comparable numbers.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cheapest way to fund working capital?

Usually the options that are not loans at all: faster collections, leaner stock and supplier terms. Among borrowing options, bank lines of credit and government-backed loans are often cheaper than online short-term products, but they are harder and slower to obtain.

When is the best time to apply?

When you do not urgently need the money. Lenders look at recent results and cash balances, so applying after a strong season or quarter usually gives you better terms and more choice.

Is factoring a sign my business is struggling?

Not necessarily. Some fast-growing and well-run B2B firms use it because their customers pay slowly. It is a tool; the question is whether its cost is justified by what the cash lets you do.
